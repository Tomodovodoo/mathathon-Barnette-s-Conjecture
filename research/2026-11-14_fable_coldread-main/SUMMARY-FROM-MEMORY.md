# The proof as I understood it, from memory

Written straight after the cold read of the PDF, without looking back at it and before opening any other project file. Mistakes are left in; they are data about the paper.

## The proof in one page

**Claim.** G cubic, bipartite, planar, 3-connected, e an edge. Then G has a Hamiltonian cycle that avoids e.

**1. Go to the dual.** The dual T of G is a triangulation of the sphere whose triangles are coloured black and white (the two classes of G). A perfect matching of G is a set P of edges of T with exactly one side of every triangle (a "selection"). The complement of the matching in G has all degrees two. It is one cycle exactly when P has no cycle, because a cycle of P in T is a cut of the complement in G, and no cycle means no cut. So I want an acyclic selection that contains e0, the edge of T crossing e.

**2. What an acyclic selection looks like.** P has k edges on k + 2 vertices, so it is two trees. Take the black triangle t0 at e0 as the outer face; its three corners are the roots. Remove e0: three trees, one root in each. Orient every edge towards its root. Every black triangle other than t0 has one edge in P, and the tail of that edge is a corner of the triangle. This gives one corner per black triangle, and every non-root vertex is used once. Such a bijection is a state s.

**3. From a state back to a selection.** Given s, the selection will use a side at s(t) in every black t, so it avoids the side opposite s(t). Call the opposite sides Q_s and add the two sides of t0 other than e0 to get S_s. If Q_s is a forest with one root in each tree (s is "good"), then S_s is a spanning tree of T. The edges of T not in S_s then give a spanning tree Y_s of the faces. Black faces have degree two in it and t0 is a leaf. Root it at t0; each black face takes the side it shares with its child. This is a selection P_s containing e0, and it leaves each chosen corner.

**4. P_s has no cycle (disk identity).** A cycle Z made of sides leaving chosen corners encloses exactly one more white face than black: W = B + 1, and its length is h = 2ℓ + 3 where ℓ counts the edges of Z whose black face is inside. Proof: Euler's formula inside Z, a count of sides with signs, and the count B = I + ℓ which uses that the state is a bijection. A selection running along Z would need W − B = h − 2ℓ = 3. Contradiction. So a good state gives a Hamiltonian cycle.

**5. A second state.** Take another state r with r(t) ≠ s(t) everywhere: a pair (r, s). Orient the side of Q_s in t from r(t) to the third corner. Every non-root vertex gets outdegree one, so every component of Q_s is a tree with one root or has exactly one directed cycle. So s is good exactly when Q_s has no cycle, and a good s has exactly one partner r.

**6. The signed count.** The edges r(t) → s(t) form disjoint directed cycles D(r, s) covering all non-root vertices. Two moves change a pair: reverse a cycle of Q_s (changes r only), or reverse a cycle of D (swaps r and s on it). Give a pair the sign ε = product over black faces of ±1 according to whether r(t) → s(t) goes counterclockwise around t. By the disk identity each such cycle has odd length, so both moves flip ε. For fixed s with a cycle in Q_s, the first move pairs up the partners of s with opposite signs. So for any function F of s, the sum of ε(r, s) F(s) over all pairs equals the sum over good states of ± F(s).

**7. The count is not zero.** With F = 1 the second move kills everything. So F must feel the direction of a D-cycle. For each white face y fix a path of faces from y to the outer face; the height of s at y adds ±1 for each black face on the path where the path turns around the chosen corner. ω(s) is the sum of the heights. Reversing a clockwise D-cycle C to counterclockwise raises ω by L(C), the number of white faces inside C, which is at least 1 by the disk identity. Take m = the least number of D-cycles over all pairs and F = ω^m. Group the pairs by the undirected cycles of D. A class with c cycles has 2^c pairs, ε alternates, ω is affine in the orientations, so the class sum is a c-fold difference of a polynomial of degree m: zero if c > m, and ± m! ∏ L(C_i) if c = m, with a sign that depends only on n and m. So the total is not zero and a good state exists, provided one pair exists.

**8. One pair.** A pair is a spanning subgraph with all degrees two of the bipartite graph "black triangle / non-root corner". That is a flow problem with capacities 2, 1, 2. The cut condition becomes e(U) − b(U) ≤ 2|U| − 4 for vertex sets U containing the roots, and this is proved by an Euler count on each component of T[U], using that every triangle of T is a face (no separating triangle).

**9. Separating triangles.** Cut T along a separating triangle into two smaller coloured triangulations, each with a cap. Solve the piece containing t0 with e0. Its solution uses exactly one side e′ of the cut triangle. Solve the other piece with e′ prescribed. The union is a selection, and it is acyclic because in each piece the three corners of the cut triangle are joined only through e′. Induction on the number of vertices finishes.

## The three things I still do not understand

1. **Why I should want Y_s to be a tree, and how anyone would come to the word "good".** Step 3 works, and I could check each line of Lemma 3.6. I could not have predicted it. The paper derives states from the target (Lemma 3.1), then says "for this graph to be a tree we need the following condition". It never says that a tree of faces forces the matching (each leaf must be matched to its neighbour, and so on inward), or that goodness is sufficient and not necessary. Page 17 says in passing that some Hamiltonian cycles do not come from good states, which told me, late, that Lemma 3.1 and Definition 3.4 are not converses. I still cannot say what a state from Lemma 3.1 that is not good looks like.

2. **What a height is, as opposed to how it is computed.** I can recite the definition (turn around the chosen corner, left is +1) and I verified the local case check in Lemma 4.6 by drawing a triangle. I cannot say in a sentence what η_s(y) measures. The proof shows that the change of height under reversal of C is the signed number of times the path from y crosses C. That is a clear statement, and it is buried in the middle of the proof. Is the height itself a crossing number of the path with something attached to s? I do not know. I also do not see why the paths go to the outer face and why the choice of paths does not matter for the conclusion.

The proof is a sequence of counts with nine letters and no picture.
