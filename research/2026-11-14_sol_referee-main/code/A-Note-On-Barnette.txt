A NOTE ON THE PROOF OF BARNETTE’S CONJECTURE
ZHENTAO LI
Abstract. Barnette’s conjecture states that every cubic 3-connected bipartite planar graph has a Hamil-
tonian cycle. We present a digested exposition of OpenAI’s generated proof of Barnette’s conjecture [3].
1. Introduction
In this note, we provide an exposition of OpenAI’s recently generated proof of Barnette’s conjecture [3]
that may be more accessible.
Definition 1. A Barnette graph is a cubic 3-connected bipartite planar graph.
Theorem 2 (Barnette’s conjecture [1]). Every Barnette graph has a Hamiltonian cycle.
The proof presented here is essentially unchanged, but replaces some parts of the generated proof with
combinatorial arguments. In particular, we replace the complex-valued exponential sum with direct bijections
(namely, two sign-reversing involutions). This note focuses solely on the proof.
We’ll prove a stronger version of the statement.
Theorem 3. For every Barnette graph G and every edge e0 ∈G, G −e0 has a Hamiltonian cycle.
For the remainder of this note, we’ll also assume G has no 3-edge cut with more than one vertex on
each side. Graphs without such cuts are called cyclically 4-edge-connected. We leave the details of the lower
connectivity case to Appendix C. So we’ll prove the following.
Theorem 4. For every cyclically 4-edge-connected Barnette graph G and every edge e0 ∈G, G −e0 has a
Hamiltonian cycle.
We now sketch the proof. In the first step, we build the face-black vertex incidence graph of G.
Definition 5. (See Figure 1.) The face-black vertex incidence graph of a planar bipartite graph G with
bipartition (B, W), written GF B, is defined as
• V (GF B) = B ∪F(G)
• E(GF B) = {bf : b ∈f}
Proof sketch. See Figure 2. Let (B, W) be the bipartition of G with vertices coloured black and white, and
e0 = b0w0 with b0 black and w0 white.
Fix a drawing of G where b0w0 is on the outer face and vertices are in general position. We find the
desired Hamiltonian cycle in G in three steps.
First we find a perfect matching M of GF B −N[b0] such that GF B −M is a spanning tree TF B of GF B.
White vertices can be thought of as faces of GF B. The planar dual of the complement of TF B is a spanning
tree of the white vertices TW . (This is the tree-cotree decomposition.)
Second, we turn TW into a spanning tree TBW of G by “tracing around it”, replacing each dual edge by
a length two path through the black vertex of the corresponding primal edge.
Finally, we orient TBW towards w0 and delete e0 and the matching consisting of all outgoing edges from
white vertices in this orientation. We claim the 2-factor that remains is a Hamiltonian cycle.
□
In what remains, we will show the cycle-breaking perfect matching and thus the spanning trees TF B exist,
that all transformations described above are well defined and produce a Hamiltonian cycle of G.
In Section 4, we prove the Key Lemma showing the existence of the matching.

1.1. Planar embedding. We close this introduction by fixing a planar embedding that we use throughout.
Another way to construct the face-black vertex incidence graph is to take G, add a vertex inside each face
connected to each black vertex on that face and delete all white vertices. We call aux(G) the graph obtained
from G by adding a vertex inside each face, connected to all black and white vertices on that face.
aux(G) is tripartite with tripartition (B, W, F(G)) and the subgraph of aux(G) induced by two parts has
exactly one vertex of the third part inside its face. So we may think of W as faces of GF B.
For the rest of this note, we fix a straight-line embedding of aux(G) where e0 is on the outer face and
vertices are in general position. All other graphs are viewed as subgraphs of aux(G) and keep this drawing.
2. Basic definitions
This section can be skipped and referenced when needed.
Let G be planar and fix a drawing of G where vertices are in general position.
A cycle C of G has a corresponding curve in the plane, separating the plane into two regions. Call one
the interior Int(C) and the other the exterior Ext(C) so Ext(C) contains the outer face. Both include the
boundary C. We abuse notation and also write Int(C) to mean the subgraph of G induced by Int(C). We
say that a vertex v is inside C if v ∈Int(C).
We can similarly define Ext(C1, . . . , Ck) for disjoint cycles C1, ..., Ck to mean the region outside all cycles.
A facial cycle of G is a cycle made of all edges of some face of G.
The planar dual of G is denoted G∗and is the graph whose vertices are F(G) and two faces (as vertices
in G∗) are adjacent if they share an edge in G.
N(v) is the set of neighbours of v, N[v] = N(v) ∪{v}.
A 2-factor of a graph G is a 2-regular spanning subgraph of G.
For a subset of vertices X of V (G), δ(X) is the set of all edges from X to V (G) \ X.
A k-edge cut is a δ(X) for some X such that |δ(X)| = k and X and V (G)−X are non-empty. A non-trivial
k-edge cut is a k-edge cut where both X and V (G) −X have at least two vertices.
For an integer x ≥0, x ⊕1 is x with its lowest binary bit flipped. i.e., it swaps 0 and 1, 2 and 3, 4 and 5,
and so on.
An involution is a function f where f(f(x)) = x everywhere. i.e., it is the identity when applied twice.
An element x such that f(x) = x is a fixed point of f and we say f fixes x.

4. Sign-reversing involutions
In this section, we prove the Key Lemma, Lemma 7. We first sketch the proof.
Proof sketch. To prove the existence of a cycle-breaking perfect matching in H = GF B −N[b0] for a rooted
Barnette graph (G, B, W, b0w0), we define two sign-reversing involutions on combinatorial objects based on
these matchings. To do so, we define
(1) some object O and partition O+, O−of O, and
(2) two involutions ι1, ι2 : O →O where non-fixed points map O+ to objects in O−(and vice-versa).
We construct ι1 so it fixes exactly the objects whose 2-factor is cycle-breaking. So if no cycle-breaking
2-factor exists, then |O+| = |O−|.
We construct ι2 so its set of fixed points is non-empty and they are all in the same part (say, O+) so
|O+| ̸= |O−|.
So together they show that some cycle-breaking matching exists, proving the lemma.
□
4.1. Combinatorial objects. We proceed with the definitions. Each 2-factor of H is a union of cycles.
Let cmin be the minimum number of cycles among all 2-factors of H.
Definition 13. A half-ray of a white vertex w is a straight upwards half-infinite line from w (in our fixed
drawing of H).
Definition 14 (of O). Each object o = (M1, M2, {x1, . . . , xcmin}) ∈O is
(1) an ordered 2-factor (M1, M2) of H, and
(2) cmin intersections between a half-ray of a white vertex w and an edge of M2.
Note the union of M1 and M2 is a 2-factor of H.
Equivalently, item 2 is cmin white vertices and M2 edges (wi, e2,i) (for i ∈{1, . . . , cmin}) where the half-ray
of each wi intersects e2,i. This second part helps us encode whether a white vertex is inside or outside a
cycle in the fixed planar drawing of H we chose.
For the remainder of this note, we restrict ourselves to the case where cmin = 1 (i.e., H is itself Hamiltonian)
and extend this to higher values of cmin in Appendix B. The extension is technical but straightforward.
Thus our objects are (M1, M2, x) where x is the intersection of a white vertex half-ray with an edge of
M2.
4.2. Motivation. Before defining O+ and O−and the involutions, we note some basic properties of pairs
of matchings we’ll need. We call the sign of an object + or - depending on its membership in O+ or O−.
Let (M1, M2) be an ordered 2-factor of H.
Recall that H is bipartite. Thus, for a cycle C of H[M1 ∪M2], we can swap the edges of M1 and M2 in C
to produce a new ordered 2-factor (M1 △C, M2 △C) where △is the symmetric difference operator defined
as X △Y = (X ∪Y ) \ (X ∩Y ). (We abuse notation and write C here instead of E(C).)
For the construction of our involutions, we often want (M1, M2, x) and (M1 △C, M2 △C, x) to have
different signs. To achieve this, we define an orientation orient(M1, M2).
Definition 15. Given an ordered 2-factor (M1, M2) of H, orient(M1, M2) is a directed graph on V (H)
where
• all edges of M1 are directed from black vertex to face-vertex, and
• all edges of M2 are directed from face-vertex to black vertex.

4.5. Properties of the involutions. By construction, ι1 and ι2 are involutions, ι1 fixes exactly the objects
(M1, M2, x) where M2 is cycle-breaking, and ι2 fixes exactly the objects (M1, M2, (w1, e2)) where M1 ∪M2
contains a single cycle C, w1 is inside C and e2 is the first edge of C the half-ray of w1 meets.
It remains to check that ι1 and ι2 are sign-reversing and all objects ι2 fixes have the same sign. Checking
ι1 and ι2 are sign-reversing is fairly routine and we leave it to Appendix A. We now check ι1 is well defined
and all objects ι2 fixes have the same sign.
4.6. Proof of well definedness of ι1. In the definition of ι1, we wrote (M1 △C, M2, x) where C is the
first cycle of H −M2. But we need to ensure M1 △C is still a perfect matching. We do this by showing C
alternates between M1-edges and non-M1-edges.
All cycles of H −M2 alternate between black and face-vertices. Each black vertex b has degree at most
2 in H −M2 since M2 is a perfect matching. Since M1 is also a perfect matching, b is incident to exactly
one M1-edge and therefore exactly one non-M1-edge in C. Again since M1 is a perfect matching from the
face-vertex side, C alternates between these edges.

Appendix C. 3-edge cuts
In this appendix, we complete the proof of the main theorem, which we now restate, by handling the case
where G has a non-trivial 3-edge cut.
Theorem 3. For every Barnette graph G and every edge e0 ∈G, G −e0 has a Hamiltonian cycle.
Proof. Let G be a minimum counterexample to Theorem 3. If G has no non-trivial 3-edge cut, by Theorem 4,
G is not a counterexample.
Let δ(X) be a non-trivial 3-edge cut and δ(X) = δB ∪δW where
• δB is all edges of δ(X) with a black end in X, and
• δW is all edges of δ(X) with a white end in X.

PAGE 13
Counting edges in G[X] from the white and black sides gives
3|B ∩X| −|δB| = 3|W ∩X| −|δW |
rearranging to
3(|B ∩X| −|W ∩X|) = |δB| −|δW |
Since the RHS is in {−3, −1, 1, 3} and the LHS is a multiple of 3, the RHS is -3 or 3. In other words, all
edges in δ(X) have the same coloured end in X, say black. If two edges of δ(X) shared an end v ∈X, then
δ(X −v) would be a 2-edge cut. So all edges of δ(X) have different ends in X, and by symmetry different
ends in V (G) −X. Hence G/X and G/(V (G) −X) are simple. If G[X] were disconnected, some component
K would have |δ(K)| ≤1, a bridge. So G[X] is connected, and by symmetry so is G[V (G) −X].
Claim 36. G1 = G/X, the graph obtained from G by contracting all of X to a single vertex x, is a Barnette
graph.
Proof of the claim. Since all ends of δ(X) in X have the same colour, G/X is bipartite. It is cubic since
|δ(X)| = 3 and all other vertex degrees are the same in G and G1. Contracting the connected subgraph
G[X] preserves planarity so G1 is planar.
Since G1 is simple and cubic, a ≤2 vertex cut Y is a ≤2 edge cut by taking the appropriate edges in
δ(Y ). Since an edge cut of G1 is an edge cut of the same size in G, G1 is 3-connected.
□
By symmetry, G2 = G/(V (G) −X) is a Barnette graph. By symmetry, we may assume e0 does not
have both ends in X and is therefore in E(G1). Both G1 and G2 are smaller than G since both sides of a
non-trivial cut have at least 2 vertices. By minimality G1 has a Hamiltonian cycle C1 missing e0. Say it also
doesn’t use e′ ∈δ(X).
By minimality, G2 has a Hamiltonian cycle C2 missing e′. Now C1 ∪C2 is a Hamiltonian cycle of G, a
contradiction.
□
