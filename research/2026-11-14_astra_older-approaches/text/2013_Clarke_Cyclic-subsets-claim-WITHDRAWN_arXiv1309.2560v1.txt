                Cyclic Subsets and Barnette's Conjecture

                                            Paul Clarke
                                  St. Paul's College Raheny
                                       September 9th 2013



                                               Abstract


            In this paper, the concept of cyclic subsets in graph theory is introduced. An
            interesting theorem which relates to the collective Hamiltonicity of these
            cyclic subsets in graphs is also presented. This paper uses this theorem to
            construct an inductive proof of Barnette's long-standing conjecture, which
            asks whether every cubic, polyhedral, bipartite graph is Hamiltonian.
            Finding a class of graphs that are certain to be Hamiltonian is one of the
            biggest unsolved problems in Hamiltonian graph theory today.


            Keywords: cyclic subset, Barnette's conjecture, graph theory, Hamiltonian
            cycle problem, induction




1. Introduction

Barnette's conjecture [6] states that every polyhedral, cubic, bipartite graph is Hamiltonian. The
conjecture relates directly to the Hamiltonian cycle problem, which is the decision problem of
determining the existence of a spanning cycle in a graph. No classifications of Hamiltonian graphs
are currently known, but many sufficient [1] and necessary [2] conditions have been discovered,
such as the theorems of Ore [4] and Dirac [3].
   Barnette's conjecture is one of a series of conjectures relating to different graph classes that are
believed to be Hamiltonian. In [5], Tait conjectured that all planar, cubic, 3-connected graphs are
Hamiltonian. Tait's conjecture was disproven by Tutte in [7] by constructing a counter-example. In
[8], Tutte then conjectured that all cubic, 3-connected, bipartite graphs are Hamiltonian. Horton
showed by a counter-example published in [9] that this conjecture was also false. Barnette's
conjecture has stood unsolved since its publication 40 years ago.
   The primary function of this paper is to present a proof of Barnette's conjecture. In doing so, I
present new results that relate to the Hamiltonicity of general graphs with regard to the new concept
of cyclic subsets introduced in this paper. These results may be useful in constructing an algorithm
for deciding the Hamiltonian cycle problem in future research.




2. Terminology

Basic graph theory terminology not defined in this paper follows Trudeau [10]. We define a graph
G = (V, E), where V is a finite non-empty set called the vertex set. The elements of V(G) are called
vertices. The elements of E(G) are two element subsets of V(G) and are called edges. Let n = |V(G)|.
Two vertices v, u are adjacent in G if and only if {v, u} ∈E(G). The number of vertices adjacent to a
vertex v, denoted deg(v), is the degree of v.
   A walk of length k in a graph G from vertex v to vertex u is a sequence of vertices (v = v0, v1,
v2, ... , vk = u) such that {vi, vi+1} ∈ E(G) for all 0 ≤ i < k. A path is a walk in which all vertices are
distinct. Two paths P1 and P2 are called vertex-independent they have no internal vertices in
common. A cycle in a graph is a path such that the start point and end point are the same vertex, or v
= u. A k-cycle is a cycle of length k.
   A graph G is connected if for every pair of vertices v and u in V(G), there exists a path in G with
v and u as start and end points. G is called k-connected if the removal of fewer than k vertices from
V(G) cannot disconnect G.
   If there exists a cycle in G that spans all of the vertices of G, G is Hamiltonian. The spanning
cycle is called a Hamiltonian cycle in G. A graph G' = (V, E) is a subgraph of G (denoted G' ⊆G) if
V(G') ⊆V(G) and E(G') ⊆E(G). Note that a graph G is Hamiltonian if and only if G contains a
subgraph that is an n-cycle.
   A graph G is planar if G can be embedded in the Euclidean plane such that no edge crossings are
formed. Areas on the plane enclosed by edges are called faces in G. G is called polyhedral if it is
planar and 3-connected. G is cubic if for every vertex v ∈V(G), deg(v) = 3. G is bipartite if V(G)
can be partitioned into two disjoint sets V1 and V2 such that every edge in E(G) connects a vertex in
V1 to a vertex in V2. If G is a cubic, polyhedral, bipartite graph, G is a Barnette graph (see fig. 1).
                   Figure 1: Example of a Barnette graph. A cyclic subset is shown in bold




   A graph K is complete if for every pair of vertices (v, u) in V(K), {v, u} ∈E(K). The complete
graph on n vertices is denoted by Kn. A complete bipartite graph on n vertices and m vertices in V1
and V2 respectively, denoted Kn, m, is a bipartite graph such that for every v ∈V1 and every u ∈V2, {v,
u} ∈E(G).
   An expansion of a graph G is a graph resulting from the subdivision of edges in G. The
subdivision of an edge e ∈ E(G) with endpoints v and u in V(G) is a graph with a vertex set
containing a new vertex w and an edge set that replaces e with two new edges: {v, w} and {w, u}.
   A vertex induced subgraph in a graph G is a subgraph G' ⊆G with a vertex set V(G') formed
from a subset of V(G) and an edge set E(G') consisting of every edge {vi, vj} ∈E(G) such that vi ∈
V(G') and vj ∈V(G'). A cyclic subset C in G is a vertex induced subgraph whereby |V(C)| = k such
that C is a k-cycle (see fig. 1).
   In this paper we consider only finite, simple, undirected graphs.




3. Cyclic subsets and Hamiltonicity

Cyclic subsets are a crucial building block of Hamiltonian graphs. In this section, I present a
theorem that is crucial to my proof of Barnette's conjecture and may in future research lead to a
complete classification of all Hamiltonian graphs.


Theorem 1: In a graph G, the union of two cyclic subsets, C1 ∪C2 = (V(C1) ∪V(C2), E(C1) ∪
E(C2)), is Hamiltonian if and only if |V(C1) ∩ V(C2)| = 2.


Sufficiency: If |V(C1) ∩ V(C2)| = 2, C1 ∪C2 is Hamiltonian.
Proof: The proposition states that if two cyclic subsets C1 and C2 in a graph G share exactly two
common vertices, C1 ∪C2 is Hamiltonian (see fig. 2).
   Suppose that we have two cyclic subsets C1 and C2 in a graph G such that V(C1) ∩ V(C2) = {vi,
vj}. As C1 is a cyclic subset, and vi, vj are adjacent, there must exist a path P1 between vi and vj such
that for every v ∈V(C1), v ∈P1. Similarly, we can construct a spanning path P2 in C2 with vi and vj as
endpoints.
   Therefore, by starting at vi, we can create a spanning cycle in C1 ∪C2 that is the union of P1 and
P2 by following P1 until we reach vj and continuing along P2 until we reach vi (see fig. 2). Therefore,
C1 ∪C2 is Hamiltonian as it contains a spanning cycle, and the proof is complete.




                    Figure 2: C1 ∪C2 is Hamiltonian if and only if |V(C1) ∩ V(C2)| = 2




Necessity: If C1 ∪C2 is Hamiltonian, |V(C1) ∩ V(C2)| = 2.
Proof: We will prove this statement by induction on n, where n = |V(C1 ∪C2)|. The smallest possible
cyclic subset is K3. As C1 ∪C2 consists of two cyclic subsets, n ≥ 4. Suppose that n = 4. As this is
the smallest possible value for n, C1 = K3 and C2 = K3. Suppose that |V(C1) ∩ V(C2)| = 1. Therefore, |
V(C1) ∪V(C2)| = 5, a clear contradiction. Note that |V(C1) ∩ V(C2)| ≠ 3, as otherwise V(C1) = V(C2).
Therefore, C1 ∪C2 is Hamiltonian only if |V(C1) ∩ V(C2)| = 2, and we have proven the base case.
   Assume that the proposition holds for n vertices in V(C1 ∪C2). Suppose now that we have C1 ∪
C2 such that |V(C1 ∪C2)| = n + 1. Take a single vertex v ∈V(C1) such that v ∉V(C1) ∩ V(C2). As v ∈
V(C1), there exist exactly two vertices v1 and v2 adjacent to v such that v1 ∈V(C1) and v2 ∈V(C1).
Remove v from V(C1) and all incident edges from E(C1). Now add {v1, v2} to E(C1).
   Clearly, C1 is still a cyclic subset. As |V(C1 ∪C2)| = n, by the induction assumption, |V(C1) ∩
V(C2)| = 2. Suppose now that we remove {v1, v2} from E(C1) and replace v along with all incident
edges. As v ∉V(C1) ∩ V(C2), |V(C1) ∩ V(C2)| = 2, and we have proven the n + 1 case.
   Therefore, by mathematical induction, the proposition holds for n ≥ 4. Therefore, if C1 ∪C2 is
Hamiltonian, |V(C1) ∩ V(C2)| = 2, and the proof is complete.


   Finally, we can state that the union of two cyclic subsets, C1 ∪C2 = (V(C1) ∪V(C2), E(C1) ∪
E(C2)), is Hamiltonian if and only if |V(C1) ∩ V(C2)| = 2.




4. Proof of Barnette's conjecture

In this section, I present a proof of Barnette's conjecture that uses the results presented in section 3.
We first however require three already established theorems. The following theorem is due to A. K.
Kelmans and can be acquired from [11].


Theorem 2 (A. K. Kelmans): Barnette's conjecture holds if and only if for any arbitrary face in a
Barnette graph, and for any arbitrary edges e1 and e2 on that face, there is a Hamiltonian cycle that
passes through e1 and avoids e2.


   We also require the following theorem of Kuratowski that relates to the planarity of graphs. The
publication can be found in [12].


Theorem 3 (K. Kuratowski): A graph is planar if and only if it does not contain a subgraph that is
an expansion of K5 or K3, 3.


   Finally, we will use a theorem of Karl Menger which relates to the connectivity of graphs. This
theorem can be found in [14].


Theorem 4 (K. Menger): Let v1 and v2 be two non-adjacent vertices in a graph G. Then the
minimum number of vertices the removal of which disconnects v1 and v2 is equal to the number of
vertex-independent paths between v1 and v2.


   We now formally state the long-standing conjecture of D. Barnette on Hamiltonian graph theory.
Theorem 5: If G is a Barnette graph, G is Hamiltonian.


   It is useful to note at this stage that the bipartitions of any Barnette graph have equal size. This
follows from the fact that all Barnette graphs are cubic. Suppose that a Barnette graph had unequal
bipartitions. Then the number of edges leaving one bipartition would not equal the number of edges
entering the other bipartition, a clear contradiction.


Proof: We will prove this statement by induction on m, where m = |V(G)| / 2. Clearly, the smallest
possible Barnette graph is one on eight vertices whereby m = 4. This is because the only possible
cubic bipartite graph whereby m = 3 is K3, 3, a well known non-planar graph.
   Let m = 4. By the announcement of [13], all Barnette graphs with fewer than 86 vertices are
Hamiltonian. Therefore, the proposition holds for all m = 4, and we have proven the base case.
   Assume that the proposition holds for |V(G)| / 2 = m. Suppose now that we have a Barnette graph
G whereby |V(G)| / 2 = m + 1 (see fig. 3). As G is 3-connected, there exists a set X of 3-tuples of
vertices in V(G) whereby for each 3-tuple t in X, the removal of t from G will disconnect G.
   Suppose now that we take a pair of adjacent vertices (v1, v2) in G such that v1 ∉t and v2 ∉t,
where t is some 3-tuple in X. As G is cubic, v1 is adjacent to two other vertices (va, vb) in G.
Similarly, v2 is adjacent to two other vertices (vc, vd). Note that va, vb, vc and vd are distinct vertices as
G is bipartite (see fig. 3).




                           Figure 3: The Barnette graph G on m+1 pairs of vertices




Lemma 1: For every pair of vertices (v1, v2) in V(G), (v1, v2) is connected to a set of four other
vertices {va, vb , vc , vd} in V(G) such that E(S) = {} or E(S) = {{va, vd}} or E(S) = {{va, vd}, {vb, vc}},
where S is the subgraph induced by {va, vb , vc , vd}.
Proof: Suppose that the proposition is not true. Therefore, there exists some pair of vertices (v1, v2)
in V(G) connected to four other vertices {va, vb , vc , vd} in V(G) such that |E(S)| ≠ {{va, vc},{vb, vd}}
or {{va, vc}} or {}.
   Suppose that E(S) = {{va, vc}, {va, vd}, {vb, vc}, {vb, vd}}. Consider the subgraph S' induced by
{v1, v2, va, vb , vc , vd}. Note that for every vertex v ∈V(S'), deg(v) = 3. Therefore, S' = K3,3. Therefore,
as S' is a subgraph of G, K3,3 is a subgraph of G. By theorem 3, this implies that G is non-planar, a
clear contradiction.
   Suppose that E(S) = {{va, vc}, {va, vd}, {vb, vc}}. Similarly, consider the subgraph S' induced by
{v1, v2, va, vb , vc , vd}. Note that E(S') = {{v1, v2}, {v1, va}, {v1, vb}, {v2, vc}, {v2, vd}, {va, vc}, {va, vd},
{vb, vc}}. Note that for every vertex v in {v1, v2, va, vc}, deg(v) = 3 in S'. Suppose now that we
remove vb and vd from V(S') and all incident edges from E(G). Note now that E(S') = {{v1, v2}, {v1,
va}, {v2, vc}, {va, vc}}.
   Note that the subgraph S* induced by {v1, v2, va, vc} is now disconnected in G. Therefore, we
have disconnected G with the removal of two vertices from V(G): vb and vd. As G is 3-connected,
this is a clear contradiction.
   Therefore, the only possible case remaining is if E(S) = {{va, vc},{va, vd}}. Consider again the
subgraph S' induced by {v1, v2, va, vb , vc , vd}. Note that E(S') = {{v1, v2}, {v1, va}, {v1, vb}, {v2, vc},
{v2, vd}, {va, vc}, {va, vd}}.
   As G is 3-connected and cubic, by theorem 4, there exist exactly three vertex-independent paths
p1, p2 and p3 between vc and vb. Note that for one of these paths p1, p1 = (vc, v2, vd, vi, vj, … vz, vb),
where for every vertex v in (vi, vj, … vz), v ∉V(S'). Let (vd, vi, vj, … vz, vb) = P1.
   Note that for one of the vertex-independent paths p2 between vc and vb, for every v in p2, v ∉
V(S'). This is because p3 = (vc, va, v1, vb), which implies that V(S') ⊆p3 ∪p1. Let p2 = P2. Clearly, by
definition, P1 and P2 are vertex-independent.
   Therefore, the subgraph induced by V(S') ∪P1 ∪P2 is an expansion of K3, 3. Therefore, by
theorem 3, G is non-planar, a clear contradiction. Therefore, E(S) ≠ {{va, vc}, {va, vd}}. As all other
cases have been proven impossible, we can now state that for every pair of vertices (v1, v2) in V(G),
(v1, v2) is connected to a set of four other vertices {va, vb , vc , vd} in V(G) such that E(S) = {} or E(S)
= {{va, vd}} or E(S) = {{va, vd}, {vb, vc}}, and the proof is complete.


   By lemma 1, E(S) = {{va, vc},{vb, vd}} or E(S) = {{va, vc}} or E(S) = {}, where S is the subgraph
induced by {va, vb , vc , vd}. Note that for every edge e adjacent to v1, v1 is on some distinct face in G.
Let the set of faces of v1 be F1. Similarly, let the set of faces of v2 be F2. Remove v1 and v2 from
V(G) and all incident edges from E(G).
   Note now that with the removal of {{v1, v2}, {v1, va}, {v1, vb}, {v2, vc}, {v2, vd}} from E(G), a
new face f is formed from the remaining vertices of the faces of F1 ∪F2. Also note that for every v ∈
{va, vb , vc , vd}, deg(v) = 2 in G. As va, vb, vc and vd now lie on the same face f, we can partition f into
three new faces by appending {va, vc} and {vb, vd} to E(G) such that va, vb, vc and vd still lie on the
same face f.
   Note now that G is cubic and bipartite. In addition, the overall structure of G has not changed.
Therefore, by our selection of (v1, v2) whereby v1 ∉t and v2 ∉t, where t ∈X, and as G is now cubic,
G is clearly still 3-connected. In order to use the induction assumption however, we are required to
prove that G is still planar.


Lemma 2: G is planar.
Proof: Suppose that G is not planar. Consider G before (v1, v2) was removed from V(G) and {va, vc},
{vb, vd} were added to E(G). Clearly, G is planar as we have recreated our Barnette graph whereby
|V(G)| / 2 = m + 1. We will prove that an edge crossing must be formed in G. Draw G such that no
edge crossings are formed. Suppose now that we remove (v1, v2) from V(G) and all adjacent edges
from E(G).
   Clearly, G must still be planar. As G is non-planar with the addition of {va, vc} and {vb, vd}, at
least one edge crossing must be formed when {va, vc} and {vb, vd} are drawn in G. Suppose now that
we replace (v1, v2) back into V(G) and all adjacent edges back into E(G).
   Consider the subgraph S' induced by {v1, v2, va, vb, vc, vd}. Note that for every vertex v in {va, vb,
vc, vd}, v is surrounded by some cycle c1 such that v ∉V(c1) (see fig. 4). This is because {va, vc} and
{vb, vd} cannot be drawn in G such that no edge crossings are formed. Therefore, if v1 lies outside
the cycle surrounding va or vb, an edge crossing will be formed by the addition of {v1, va} and {v1,
vb} to E(G). Similarly for v2.
   Suppose that v1 and v2 do not lie outside the cycles surrounding va, vb, vc and vd. Note that va and
vb are surrounded by a different cycle c2 to vc and vd, as the addition of {va, vc} and {vb, vd} forces an
edge crossing in G. Therefore, the addition of {v1, v2} to E(G) must form an edge crossing in G, as
v1 and v2 are therefore surrounded by different cycles c1 and c2 in G.
   Therefore, in all possible cases, an edge crossing is formed with the addition of (v1, v2) to V(G)
and all adjacent edges to E(G). Therefore, the original Barnette graph on m + 1 pairs of vertices
cannot be planar, a clear contradiction. Therefore, G must be planar, and the proof is complete.


   By lemma 2, G is planar. Therefore, as G is also cubic, 3-connected and bipartite, G is a Barnette
graph. Note also that |V(G)| / 2 = m. By the induction assumption, G is Hamiltonian, and therefore
Barnette's conjecture holds. As {va, vc} and {vb, vd} lie on the same face f, by theorem 2, there exists
some Hamiltonian cycle H in G that uses {va, vc} and avoids {vb, vd}. Let the n-cycle subgraph
formed by the vertices of H be C1.




        Figure 4: If G is not planar, the original Barnette graph cannot be drawn without edge crossings




   Suppose now that we add {v1, v2} back into V(G) and all adjacent edges back into E(G). Note
that the subgraph induced by {v1, v2, va, vc} is a cyclic subset. Call this cyclic subset C2. Suppose
now that for every edge e ∈E(G), remove e from E(G) if e ∉E(C1) or if e ∉E(C2). C1 is now a
cyclic subset such that V(C1) = V(G) / {v1, v2}. Note also that V(C1) ∩ V(C2) = {va, vc}. By theorem
1, C1 ∪C2 is Hamiltonian (see fig. 5).




         Figure 5: C1 and C2 share exactly two common vertices: va and vc. Therefore, G is Hamiltonian


   Let the Hamiltonian cycle in C1 ∪C2 be H'. Clearly, H' cannot use {va, vc}, as otherwise either va
or vc will be visited twice in H'. Suppose now that we replace all removed edges back into E(G).
Remove {va, vc} and {vb, vd} from E(G). Note that G is now the original Barnette graph whereby
|V(G)| / 2 = m + 1. As H' does not use {va, vc} or {vb, vd}, there exists some Hamiltonian cycle
through the original Barnette graph on m + 1 vertices.
   Therefore, we have proven the m + 1 case. By mathematical induction, the proposition holds for
all m ≥ 4. As this encapsulates all possible Barnette graphs, the proof is complete.
