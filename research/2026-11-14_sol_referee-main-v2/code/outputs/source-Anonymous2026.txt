A CONSTRUCTIVE PROOF OF BARNETTE’S CONJECTURE
Abstract. We give an explicit constructive proof of Barnette’s conjecture,
developing the matching argument of OpenAI’s September 2026 preprint. For
every finite simple cubic bipartite 3-connected planar graph, we construct a
Hamiltonian cycle avoiding any prescribed edge. Our enumeration algorithm
uses polynomial space and O(poly(N)3N/4) time on a graph with N vertices.
The central step is to find a spanning tree with prescribed degrees in a rooted
vertex–face incidence graph. We characterize the exact separating-triangle
obstruction to this tree and prove that every component of the graph of local
matching exchanges contains a pair that yields the required tree. A signed
integer polynomial proves this reachability.
A deterministic walk between
marked matching pairs realizes the cancellation through local moves, while
a determinant count bounds the faster enumeration. The exposition is self-
contained and follows the cube from matchings to trees to a Hamiltonian
cycle.
1. Introduction
A Hamiltonian cycle visits every vertex of a graph exactly once. Barnette’s
conjecture asserts that every finite simple cubic bipartite 3-connected planar graph
has such a cycle. The difficulty is connectivity. A spanning subgraph of degree two
at every vertex is a disjoint union of cycles. The task is to make it connected.
We give a constructive exposition of the matching proof in OpenAI’s September
2026 preprint [11]. The construction passes through a spanning tree in a vertex–
face incidence graph. We characterize exactly when this tree exists and give two
explicit constructions. Duality and a recursive gluing procedure then recover the
Hamiltonian cycle, with a prescribed edge omitted.
Theorem 1.1 (Constructing a Hamiltonian cycle). Given a finite simple cubic
bipartite 3-connected planar graph G on N vertices and an edge e, a deterministic
algorithm constructs a Hamiltonian cycle of G avoiding e. The algorithm uses
polynomial space and O(poly(N)3N/4) time.
Here poly(N) denotes a factor bounded by a polynomial in N. The prescribed
edge fixes the boundary data needed to join solutions across a separating triangle in
the dual triangulation.
The cube shows why degree two does not ensure connectivity. The boundaries
of two opposite square faces form a spanning subgraph of degree two with two
components. A different choice of edges gives one Hamiltonian cycle (Figure 1). We
follow the cube from its plane dual to the final cycle.

Theorem 1.2 (The incidence-tree criterion). Let T be a simple sphere triangulation
with at least four vertices, with faces properly colored black and white and a chosen
black outer face. For its incidence graphs H and B, the following are equivalent:
(i) There is a perfect matching s of B for which H −s is a spanning tree.
(ii) The graph B has two edge-disjoint perfect matchings.
(iii) No separating triangle has black inner boundary.
Equivalently, H has a spanning tree with degrees two at the bounded black centers,
three at the outer center, dH(x) −1 at nonroots, and dH(v) at roots. A perfect
matching is a tree matching if and only if it has exactly one disjoint perfect matching
in B.
The criterion explains when to split the triangulation. A flow computation tests
condition (ii). If the test succeeds, the whole rooted triangulation can be treated
at once. If it fails, a black inner-boundary separating triangle supplies a certified
obstruction and a place to cut. A separating triangle with white inner boundary
does not force this step.

Suppose B has two edge-disjoint perfect matchings (r, s). They supply two
kinds of cycle reversal, defined in Section 4. Every connected component of the
resulting exchange graph contains a pair whose second matching is a tree matching
(Theorem 5.2). The proof compares two expansions of a signed integer polynomial:
one cancels the cyclic configurations, while the other proves that some terms survive.
We also turn the cancellation into a deterministic local walk (Theorem 5.3).
Ordered marks record the weight choices, and two involutions guide the walk
through matching pairs. Each phase either reaches a tree matching or ends at a
pair whose matching union has at least two fewer cycles. Each phase follows a finite
path, and the decrease at restarts proves termination.
The faster algorithm searches only the matchings s. A partial assignment is
rejected when it cannot extend to a perfect matching or when deleting its spokes
has already disconnected H. The disk identity underlying the exchanges also shows
that all perfect matchings of B have the same permutation sign. Their number is
therefore the absolute value of a determinant, at most 3n/2 by Hadamard’s inequality.
Since n = N/2 −1, this gives the bound in Theorem 1.1.
Barnette’s conjecture was recorded in the 1969 Waterloo proceedings [8]. Tutte’s
counterexample [14] shows that the bipartite hypothesis cannot be omitted. Goodey
proved the conjecture when every face has size four or six [6]; Schnieders extended the
allowed face sizes to eight [12]. Earlier tree-based constructions and their algorithmic
properties are developed in [2]. For the history and equivalent formulations, see
[1, 7].
The disk identity, the positive-circulation argument, and the recovery of the two
induced trees come from [11]and are proved here. We express the source’s complex
exponential sum as an integer polynomial and carry out the cancellation in each
exchange component. We also obtain the exact rooted criterion and the bound
for single-matching enumeration. The marked walk implements the cancellation
by the involution principle of Garsia and Milne [5]. The incidence matchings are
the Tutte states of trinity theory [15, 9]. Kenyon, Propp, and Wilson [10] give a
related correspondence between plane spanning trees and perfect matchings of an
associated graph.
Eppstein’s algorithm for graphs of maximum degree three [3] has the faster bound
O(2N/3) and uses linear space. Of our two constructions, enumeration gives the
smaller proved bound, while the marked walk makes the cancellation into a sequence
of local moves. Both recover a cycle through the same incidence-tree certificate.

Proposition 2.1 (Disjoint mates). A perfect matching s of B has a disjoint mate

point towards that cycle.

a perfect matching r disjoint from s. Rooted tree orientations are forced, while each
unicyclic component has two choices. These choices are independent.

Proof. Take any two perfect matchings r, s, now allowing common edges. In each

from the sign ε of an ordered pair in (8).
Fixing a partial matching deletes its rows and columns from KB. Its extensions
correspond to the perfect matchings of the remaining matrix, and their determinant
terms again have a common sign. The absolute determinant of that matrix therefore
counts the extensions. Ordinary bipartite matching tests suffice below; determinants
provide an alternative exact extension test.
The input to the enumeration algorithm is a rooted triangulation satisfying
the equivalent conditions of Theorem 1.2, with its bounded black faces ordered as
t1, . . . , tn. It returns a tree matching and its unique mate. A partial assignment
chooses distinct incident nonroots for the first j −1 faces; identify it with the
corresponding set of deleted spokes S. Start with (S, j) = (∅, 1) and apply the
following recursion, stopping at the first returned pair.

1. If j = n + 1, put s = S. Direct the three components of Qs towards their roots,
and let r(t) be the tail of the edge supplied by t. Return (r, s).
2. Otherwise, try each unused nonroot corner x of tj in a fixed order, and put
S′ = S ∪{(tj, x)}.
3. Reject S′ if H −S′ is disconnected, or if the unused faces and nonroots have
no perfect matching. Otherwise recurse with (S′, j + 1). If every candidate is
rejected or leads to a failed recursive call, backtrack to the preceding assignment.
Connectivity is a safe prefix test because deleting more spokes cannot reconnect a
graph. Equivalently, the duals of the deleted spokes must remain acyclic, by com-
plementary plane-tree duality as recalled in Section 7.1. At a complete assignment
H −s has 2n + 4 vertices and 2n + 3 edges, so connectedness makes it a tree. The
prefix tests give necessary conditions for a tree completion; backtracking handles
branches that pass the tests but do not lead to one.
Proposition 6.2. This search returns a tree matching. It runs in O(n3M) time
and polynomial space, and hence in O(n33n/2) time.
Proof. A tree matching exists by Theorem 1.2. None of its prefixes is rejected: its
remaining edges give a matching extension, and H −S′ contains the final tree. The
finite search must therefore find a complete assignment.
Every retained prefix extends to some perfect matching, whether or not it extends
to a tree. Across all M matchings there are at most (n + 1)M different prefixes.
There are at most three child tests per retained prefix. A residual perfect-matching
test takes O(n2) operations by at most n augmentations in a graph with O(n) edges;
connectivity takes O(n). This gives O(n3M) time. Depth-first traversal stores a
path of length n together with polynomial working data.
□
The implementation first tests the second matching supplied by max flow. If it is
already a tree matching, enumeration is unnecessary. Otherwise its corners may be
tried first in the search. This changes the order, not the correctness or the bound.
7. Recovering the Hamiltonian cycle
We now turn a tree matching into two induced trees of T, with the prescribed
outer edge in one tree. The construction comes from [11, Sections 6–7]. It requires
only a tree matching, so it also works in the presence of separating triangles with
white inner boundary.
7.1. A tree on the white faces. Fix a tree matching s, an edge e0 of t0, and the
white triangle y0 across that edge. The faces of H correspond to the white triangles
of T: erasing the original edges of T joins each white triangle to the three black
sectors across its sides. Since H −s is a spanning tree, the duals of the deleted
spokes form a spanning tree Ws on the white faces.
Here we use complementary plane-tree duality: the edges complementary to a
spanning tree dualize to a spanning tree. To see this, contract the primal tree. The
dual of the resulting one-vertex plane graph consists exactly of the complementary
dual edges. It is connected, and Euler’s formula gives one fewer edge than vertices.
More generally, the complement of a spanning forest dualizes to a connected spanning
subgraph: extend the forest to a tree and apply the preceding fact.
Root Ws at y0 and assign each tree edge to its endpoint farther from y0. A
deleted spoke (t, s(t)) borders the white triangles across the two sides of t incident

Lemma 7.1. The spanning subgraph (V (T), P) is a forest with two components.
Its components are the two induced trees of a partition of V (T), with the ends of e0
in one class and the third vertex of t0 in the other.
Proof. Put P0 = P −{e0} and orient its selected side in each bounded black triangle
t from s(t). Outdegrees are one at nonroots and zero at roots. An undirected cycle
would be directed: its edges have distinct tails among the cycle vertices, so every
cycle vertex has one outgoing cycle edge. For its bounded disk use Bd, Wd, h, l as in

boundary side contributes the sign of its interior face. Ordinary signed side counting

The outdegree count now shows that every component of P0 contains exactly one
root. Adding e0 joins two distinct root components, leaving the third root in the
other component. Hence P is a forest with (n + 3) −(n + 1) = 2 components.
The edges complementary to P dualize to a connected spanning subgraph of T ∗.
Every face has two unselected sides, so this subgraph is 2-regular and hence one
Hamiltonian cycle. Draw it as a Jordan curve crossing exactly the unselected edges
of T, once each. The ends of an edge lie on the same side precisely when that edge
is selected. Each component of P lies on one side, and both sides contain vertices
because an unselected edge crosses the curve once. Since P has two components,
they are exactly the two sides. Every edge with both ends in one component is
selected, so the components are induced trees. The preceding root-component count
gives the required placement of the vertices of t0.

7.3. Cutting only when the matching problem is obstructed. If the flow in

If it fails, Theorem 1.2 supplies a black inner-boundary separating triangle. It can
be found by enumerating 3-cycles, testing separation and the three boundary face
colors; all these operations are polynomial. Split along it, capping both sides by a
facial triangle. The color of each cap is forced. Signed side counting makes three
times the face-color balance on either side a sum of three signs; divisibility by three
forces all three signs to agree. The cap receives the opposite color from those three
adjacent faces.
First solve the piece containing the prescribed face t0, with prescription e0, and
label its two induced-tree classes 0 and 1. The cap has both labels: putting all three
vertices in one class would give a cycle in that induced tree. Prescribe its unique

and white if needed, and solve with this prescription. Rename the two tree labels
to agree on the common triangle. For each label the two induced trees intersect in
one vertex or one edge with its ends, so their union is a tree. It remains induced
because no edge joins the strict interiors of opposite sides of the separator. The
original prescription is preserved.

8. Implementation and further questions
The companion implementation follows the objects in the proof. Its default con-
structor first tries the matching produced by integral flow, then searches extendable
matching prefixes while preserving connectivity of H −S. Alternative entry points
perform breadth-first search, a uniform local random walk, or the marked involution


CONSTRUCTING HAMILTONIAN CYCLES
15
walk proved in Theorem 5.3. The companion gives the implementation details and
the proofs for the breadth-first and random-walk alternatives. Enumeration and the
marked walk are independent constructions of a tree matching.
The returned certificate is a cyclic ordering of the original vertices. A separate
checker tests that every vertex occurs exactly once, every consecutive pair, including
the last and first, is an input edge, and the prescribed edge is absent. It uses none of
the matching or plane-dual construction. This allows a returned cycle to be checked
independently of the procedure that found it.
The companion also records exact examples of the obstruction, the determinant
count, and the cube recovery. Computational checks test the implementation and
illuminate the objects; the proofs above supply the arbitrary-order statements.
The construction leaves three quantitative questions. How large can the distance
to a tree pair be in an exchange component? How many tree matchings occur
relative to all matchings, and what governs the local walk’s hitting time? Can the
prescribed-degree structure support stronger pruning than the two elementary prefix
tests? The component theorem answers the reachability question, and a determinant
counts all incidence matchings. Neither result alone gives a polynomial bound on
local distance or on construction time.
