THE PROOF OF BARNETTE’S CONJECTURE: AN EXPOSITION
Abstract. We give a self-contained exposition of OpenAI’s 2026 proof of Barnette’s conjecture, in the
form that every cubic, bipartite, 3-connected planar graph has a Hamiltonian cycle avoiding any given
edge. In a cubic graph the complement of a perfect matching has degree two at every vertex, so the task
is to find a perfect matching whose complement is connected. In the planar dual, a triangulation whose
triangles are coloured black and white, a perfect matching corresponds to a set of edges containing
exactly one side of every triangle; the matching’s complement in the original graph is connected exactly
when this set contains no cycle. We reduce the search for such a set to a choice of one corner in each
black triangle. A suitable choice exists because of a signed count over pairs of choices, in which the
unsuitable ones cancel and a second grouping of the same sum shows that it is not zero. The avoided
edge is what lets an induction cut the triangulation along triangles that are not faces.
1. Introduction
A Hamiltonian cycle is a connected spanning subgraph in which every vertex has degree two. In a cubic
graph the degree condition is the easy half. Deleting a perfect matching leaves every vertex with two
edges, and a cubic bipartite graph has a perfect matching by Hall’s theorem. Connectivity is the whole
problem. On the cube, the two opposite squares form a subgraph of degree two with two components
(Figure 1(a)).
Theorem 1.1 (OpenAI [14, Theorem 1.1 and Corollary 7.1]). Let G be a finite simple graph that is
cubic, bipartite, planar and 3-connected, and let e be an edge of G. Then G has a Hamiltonian cycle that
does not contain e.
Here 3-connected means that G has at least four vertices and stays connected after deleting any two of
them. Barnette’s conjecture is the case without e. The edge e is there for the induction of Section 6,
which cuts G into smaller graphs and needs their cycles to agree on the cut. That the version with e is
equivalent to the conjecture goes back to Kelmans, as explained by Hertel [9, Corollary 3] and by Gorsky,
Steiner and Wiederrecht [8, Theorem 4.3].
The plan. The planar dual T has a vertex in each face of G and an edge across each edge of G. Each
vertex of G lies in a triangular face of T, coloured by its bipartition class (Figure 1(b)). A perfect
matching of G selects one side of every triangle. Its complement is connected exactly when the selected
sides contain no cycle: a cycle would separate the triangles inside it from those outside (Section 2). We
seek such an acyclic selection containing the side e0 that crosses e.
(1) Corner choices. Fix a black triangle t0 at e0. Removing e0 from the desired selection leaves three
trees, each containing one corner of t0. Direct them towards these corners. In each other black
triangle the selected side then points away from one corner, its tail, and every vertex off t0 is a
tail exactly once. Such a choice of corners is a state (Section 3).
(2) From a state back to a selection. The side we take must touch the chosen corner, so it must avoid
the opposite side. Call a state good when these opposite sides form three trees, each containing
one corner of t0 (Figure 1(c)). The sides that are not avoided then join the triangles into a tree,
and this tree forces a selection (panels (d)–(e)). A count called the disk identity proves that this
selection has no cycle.
(3) Two states. Another state, different in every triangle, orients the opposite sides with one edge
leaving every vertex off t0. This makes a state good as soon as its opposite sides contain no cycle
(Section 4).
(4) One count, two groupings. Give each pair a sign and a weight depending only on its second state.
A reversal pairs off pairs whose second state is unsuitable, with opposite signs and equal weights.
The arrows between the two chosen corners form cycles (Figure 1(f)). Reversing a clockwise cycle
flips the sign and raises the weight by the number of white triangles inside the cycle, which is at
least one. Grouped by these cycles, with the weight raised to a suitable power, the sum becomes
a sum of products of these numbers, all of one sign. So the sum is not zero, and a good state
survives.

History and sources. We follow Hertel’s survey [9] for the older history. Tait conjectured in 1884 that
cubic 3-connected planar graphs are Hamiltonian, and Tutte disproved this in 1946 with a non-bipartite
example [17]. Barnette asked whether bipartiteness rescues the statement. Before 2026 the conjecture
was known when all faces have four or six sides [9], under conditions on two of the three face colour
classes [5], and when all faces have at most eight sides [15]. Computer checks reached 64 vertices in work
of Holton, Manvel and McKay [9], and 90 vertices in work of Brinkmann, Goedgebeur and McKay [3]. In
a preprint dated 24 September 2026, OpenAI proved Theorem 1.1 [14]. The paired-state argument, disk
identity, flow argument and induction are theirs. States are the corner choices of Tutte’s theory of trinity,
as explained by Hine and Kálmán [10]. The target, two induced trees in T, is Stein’s reformulation [16]
in the form of Alt, Payne, Schmidt and Wood [2, Lemma 5]. Two expositions followed, one by Li [12],
who works in G, and one in an unsigned manuscript [4], which adds an exact criterion for the first step
and an enumeration algorithm. Remark 4.10 and Section 7 say how these accounts and ours differ.

2. The dual triangulation and the target
The dual. Fix an embedding of G in the sphere, with edges drawn as polygonal arcs. Its dual T has a
vertex in each face of G and an edge crossing each edge of G once. Conversely G is the graph of faces of
T: each face of T contains one vertex of G, and two faces are adjacent in G when they share a side.
Lemma 2.1. Let G be as in Theorem 1.1. Then T is a simple graph with at least four vertices, every
face of T is bounded by a triangle, and the faces of T can be coloured black and white so that every edge
of T has one black and one white face.
Proof. First, G has no set of at most two edges whose removal separates a nonempty vertex set U from
the nonempty rest. If |U| = 1 then three edges leave U, and if |U| = 2 at least four do, since G is simple.
So |U| ≥3, and likewise for the rest. Deleting the at most two endpoints in U of the cut edges leaves a
nonempty part of U with no edge to the rest, contradicting 3-connectivity.
A loop of T, or two edges of T joining the same two vertices, is a closed curve that meets G in one
or two points, on one or two edges of G. The two ends of a crossed edge lie on opposite sides of the
curve, so both sides contain vertices of G, and the crossed edges form a cut of size at most two. Hence
T is simple. The face of T containing a vertex v of G is bounded by a closed walk through the three
faces of G at v, and in a simple graph a closed walk of length three is a triangle. Euler’s formula gives
|V (T)| = |F(G)| = 2 + |E(G)| −|V (G)| = 2 + |V (G)|/2 ≥4. Finally, colour the face of T containing v by
the class of v in the bipartition of G. The two faces at an edge of T contain the two ends of an edge of G,
so they get different colours.
□
From now on T is a coloured triangulation: a simple graph with at least four vertices, embedded in
the sphere so that every face is bounded by a triangle, with a black and white colouring of its faces in
which every edge has one face of each colour. Two faces share at most one side, since two faces with two

common sides would have the same three corners, and that triangle would bound both of them, leaving no
room for a fourth vertex. Euler’s formula needs a connected graph. The next lemma supplies connectivity
for T, for the inside of a cycle in Section 3 and for the pieces of Section 6.
Lemma 2.2. Let R be a finite graph embedded in the sphere such that every face of R is bounded by a
cycle of R. Then R is connected.
Proof. Let K be a component of R. The boundary of a face is a cycle, which is connected, so it lies in
one component. Let Ωbe the union of K and the faces whose boundary lies in K, and Ω′ the union of
the other components and the other faces. A point of K has a neighbourhood that meets only K and
faces with that point on their boundary, and these faces have their boundary in K. So Ωis open, and Ω′
is open for the same reason. They are disjoint, they cover the sphere, which is connected, and Ωis not
empty. Hence Ω′ is empty and R = K.
□
Each edge of T has exactly one black face and each black face has three sides, so the number of edges
is three times the number of black faces, and likewise for white faces. With Euler’s formula this gives

The target. A perfect matching of G is a set of edges of G meeting every vertex once. Read in T, it is a
set of edges of T containing exactly one side of every face.
Definition 2.4. A selection is a set P of edges of T that contains exactly one side of every face of T.
We write P ∗for the set of edges of G that cross the edges of P.
A selection matches each black face with the white face across its selected side, so it has k edges. The
question is which selections leave a connected graph in G. We need the answer for two kinds of edge set,
a selection here and a spanning tree in Section 3, so the next lemma is about an arbitrary set N of edges.
For a plane graph M and N ⊆E(M) write M ∗−N ∗for the spanning subgraph of the dual M ∗whose
edges cross the edges not in N.
Lemma 2.5 (duality). Let M be a connected plane graph and N ⊆E(M).
(1) If N contains a cycle Z, then an edge of M ∗joins a face inside Z to a face outside Z exactly
when it crosses an edge of Z, and M ∗−N ∗is disconnected.
(2) If N is acyclic, then M ∗−N ∗is connected. If N is a spanning tree of M, then M ∗−N ∗is a
spanning tree of M ∗.
Proof. (1) By the Jordan curve theorem Z splits the sphere into two connected open regions with boundary
Z, and every face of M lies in one of them. An edge of M ∗crosses only its own edge of M, so it joins
faces on opposite sides of Z exactly when it crosses an edge of Z. The two faces at an edge of Z lie on
opposite sides, so both sides contain faces, and every edge of M ∗between the two sides crosses an edge of
N and is missing from M ∗−N ∗.
(2) Suppose M ∗−N ∗is disconnected, and let Φ be the set of faces in one of its components. Call an
edge of M a boundary edge if one of its faces is in Φ and the other is not. A boundary edge lies in N,
since otherwise the edge of M ∗crossing it would leave Φ. Around a vertex v edges and faces alternate,
and an edge at v is a boundary edge exactly when the faces before and after it differ in membership of Φ.
Membership changes an even number of times around a circle, so every vertex meets an even number of
boundary edges. There is a boundary edge, for otherwise all faces at each vertex would agree, the two
ends of every edge would agree because they share the faces of that edge, and connectivity of M would
put all faces in Φ or none. A nonempty edge set with all degrees even contains a cycle, and this cycle
lies in N. If N is a spanning tree, then M ∗−N ∗is connected with |E(M)| −|V (M)| + 1 = |F(M)| −1
edges by Euler’s formula, so it is a spanning tree of M∗.
□
A reader who knows plane duality may prefer the classical proof of (2): contract a spanning tree
containing N to one vertex, which deletes the crossing edges from the dual and leaves the dual connected.
Proposition 2.6. Let P be an acyclic selection of a coloured triangulation T with dual G.
(1) The edges of G not in P ∗form a Hamiltonian cycle of G.
(2) P is a forest with exactly two components, and an edge of T lies in P exactly when its two ends
lie in the same component.

Proof. (1) Every face of T has one side in P, so every vertex of G meets one edge of P ∗, and since G is
cubic the other edges give every vertex degree two. By Lemma 2.5(2) with M = T and N = P they form
a connected graph, and a finite connected graph with all degrees two is a single cycle.
(2) P is acyclic with k edges on k + 2 vertices, so it has two components. Suppose an edge uv /∈P has
both ends in one component. Then P ∪{uv} contains a cycle Z through uv, and by Lemma 2.5(1) the
edges of G crossing Z form an edge cut of G. The Hamiltonian cycle of (1) meets this cut in exactly one
edge, the one crossing uv, because the other edges of Z are in P. But a cycle meets every edge cut in an
even number of edges.
□
Part (1) shows that the two conditions on the target do different jobs: one side per face gives degree
two, and acyclicity gives connectivity. Part (2) is the two-induced-trees form [16, 2], and we use it in
Lemma 3.1 and in the gluing. An acyclic selection containing an edge e0 of T gives a Hamiltonian cycle
of G that avoids the edge crossing e0, so Theorem 1.1 asks for an acyclic selection containing a prescribed
edge.
3. Corner choices and the tree of faces
Fix a face t0 of T and one of its sides e0 = ab, and let o be the third corner of t0. Exchanging the
colours if necessary, t0 is black; selections do not depend on the colours. We draw t0 as the outer face, so
that “inside” a cycle means on the side away from t0. The corners a, b, o are the roots. Let A be the set of
black faces other than t0 and X the set of vertices other than the roots. By (2.3), |A| = |X| = k −1 =: n.
Where the corner choices come from. Suppose P is an acyclic selection containing e0, and put
P0 = P \ {e0}.
Lemma 3.1. Each of the three components of P0 contains exactly one root. Orient every edge of P0
towards the root of its component, and for t ∈A let s(t) be the tail of the side of t in P0. Then s is a
bijection from A to X, and s(t) is a corner of t for every t.
Proof. By Proposition 2.6(2), a and b lie in one component of P and o in the other, because ao /∈P.
Deleting e0 splits the first component into a tree containing a and a tree containing b. In a tree oriented
towards its root every vertex other than the root has exactly one outgoing edge, so every vertex of X is
the tail of exactly one edge of P0, and the roots are tails of none. The side of t ∈A in P is not e0, which
lies in t0, so s(t) ∈X is defined. Distinct black faces have distinct selected sides, because an edge has
only one black face, and distinct edges of P0 have distinct tails, so s is injective; it is onto because every
x ∈X is the tail of an edge of P0, whose black face lies in A.
□
So the unknown is a choice of one corner in every face of A that uses every vertex of X once.
Definition 3.2. A state is a bijection s: A →X such that s(t) is a corner of t for every t ∈A.
We will build P from a state, and P will contain, in every t ∈A, one of the two sides of t at s(t). The
third side of t, opposite s(t), is excluded (Figure 3). Let Qs be the set of these opposite sides, one in
each face of A; they are distinct because an edge has only one black face, so |Qs| = n. In t0 the selection
must contain e0 and exclude ao and bo. Put Ss = Qs ∪{ao, bo}, the set of edges that every such selection
excludes; it has n + 2 = |V (T)| −1 elements.

For a good state Lemma 3.6 produces a selection Ps containing e0 without any further choice. It
remains to see that Ps has no cycle. A cycle in Ps \ {e0} would be made of sides leaving chosen corners,
and the rest of this section says what such a cycle looks like.
The inside of a cycle. Throughout the paper, the cycles we need are made of sides leaving corners
chosen by a state. Let r be a state, and suppose that in every face t ∈A one of the two sides at r(t) has
been chosen and directed out of r(t). We call these the chosen sides. The state is called r here because
Section 4 applies these facts to the first state of a pair.
Lemma 3.7 (outdegree). Let ⃗E be a set of edges of T, each directed, such that every vertex has at most
one outgoing edge in ⃗E. Then every cycle of ⃗E is a directed cycle. A component of ⃗E with ν vertices,
of which γ have no outgoing edge, has ν −γ edges. So γ ≤1, the component is a tree if γ = 1, and it
contains exactly one cycle if γ = 0.
Proof. A cycle with h edges has h distinct tails on its h vertices, so every vertex is the tail of one of its
edges. The edge count is the sum of the outdegrees, and a connected graph with ν vertices has at least
ν −1 edges, with equality for a tree, and exactly one cycle if it has ν.
□
The chosen sides have outdegree one at every vertex of X and zero at the roots, so Lemma 3.7 applies.

Euler’s formula needs a connected plane graph, so we first say what the inside of Z is. Let RZ consist of
Z and the vertices and edges of T inside Z. By the Jordan curve theorem the outside of Z is a connected
open region with boundary Z; it contains no point of RZ, so it is one face of RZ, bounded by the cycle
Z. Every other face of RZ lies inside Z and contains no vertex or edge of T, so it lies in one face of T;
conversely a face of T inside Z is a connected region disjoint from RZ whose boundary triangle lies in RZ.
So the faces of RZ are the B + W faces of T inside Z and the outside of Z, each bounded by a cycle, and
RZ is connected by Lemma 2.2. We also use that every face at a vertex strictly inside Z lies inside Z.
Lemma 3.8 (disk identity). Let r be a state and Z a directed cycle of chosen sides for r, of length h.
Let B and W be the numbers of black and white faces inside Z, and ℓthe number of edges of Z whose
black face is inside. Then W = B + 1 and h = 2ℓ+ 3. In particular h is odd and Z encloses at least one
white face.
Proof. Two counts hold for every cycle of T. The plane graph RZ has I + h vertices, B + W + 1 faces,
and (3(B + W) + h)/2 edges, since each inner face has three sides, an edge strictly inside lies on two inner
faces and an edge of Z on one; Euler’s formula gives the first count below. For the second, count each
side of an inner face with +1 if the face is black and −1 if it is white: an edge strictly inside contributes
+1 −1, and an edge of Z contributes +1 or −1 according to its inner face. Thus
B + W = 2I + h −2,
3(B −W) = 2ℓ−h.
The third count uses the state. Send each black face t inside Z to r(t), a vertex inside or on Z. This
is injective because r is. Every vertex v strictly inside is hit: v = r(t) for some t ∈A because r is onto
X, and t has the corner v, so it lies inside. A vertex v on Z is r(t) for exactly one t, and the edge of Z
leaving v is the chosen side of that t, because each face has only one chosen side and r is injective. So v
is hit exactly when that edge has its black face inside. This happens for ℓvertices of Z, so
B = I + ℓ.
Substituting into the first count gives W = I + h −ℓ−2, hence B −W = 2ℓ−h + 2, and the second
count becomes 3(2ℓ−h + 2) = 2ℓ−h, that is h = 2ℓ+ 3. Then W −B = h −2ℓ−2 = 1.

4. Pairs of states
We do not know how to point to a good state. This section shows that one exists, by a count in four
steps. A second corner choice turns goodness into the absence of cycles (Lemma 4.2). Every pair of
choices gets a sign, and the pairs that fail cancel in twos (Lemma 4.5). A weight, built from the white
faces that certain cycles enclose, lets the same sum be computed in a second way (Lemma 4.6). The
second computation shows that the sum is not zero (Proposition 4.8), so some pair does not fail.

condition concerns whole components. Another state r that differs from s in every face reduces the second

5. The first pair
Proposition 4.8 needs one pair. Let B be the bipartite graph on A ∪X in which t is joined to its
corners in X. States are the perfect matchings of B. A spanning subgraph of B with all degrees two is a
union of disjoint even cycles, and taking alternate edges along each cycle splits it into two states that
differ in every face, that is, a pair. We find such a subgraph as a flow.
Give the edges of a network capacity 2 from a source to each x ∈X, capacity 1 from x to each face

Theorem 6.2. Let T be a coloured triangulation, t0 a face of T and e0 a side of t0. Then T has an
acyclic selection containing e0.
Proof. We use induction on the number of vertices. If T has no separating triangle, exchange the colours
if necessary so that t0 is black. Then T has a pair by Proposition 5.2, a good state by Proposition 4.8,
and an acyclic selection containing e0 by Proposition 3.9.
Otherwise let ∆= xyz be a separating triangle, let D1 be the closed disk bounded by ∆that contains
t0 and D2 the other, and let T1, T2 be the pieces, coloured as in Lemma 6.1. The face t0 is a face of T1,
and by induction T1 has an acyclic selection P1 containing e0. The cap of T1 is bounded by ∆, so P1
contains exactly one side of ∆. Call it e′. The cap of T2 is also bounded by ∆, and by induction T2 has
an acyclic selection P2 containing e′, which is then its only side of ∆. Put P = P1 ∪P2.
P is a selection containing e0. Every face of T is a face of exactly one piece. Say f is a face of T1.
The edges that T1 and T2 share are the sides of ∆, and P2 contains only e′ among them, which is also in
P1. So f has the same sides in P as in P1, namely exactly one, and the same holds for faces of T2.
P is acyclic. By Proposition 2.6(2) applied in Ti, a side of ∆other than e′ has its ends in different
components of Pi. So if e′ = xy, then x and y lie in one component of Pi and z in the other, and the
only path in Pi between two of x, y, z is the edge e′. Suppose Z is a cycle in P. It is not contained in P1
or in P2, so it contains an edge of P2 \ P1, which is not a side of ∆and so has an end strictly inside D2.
Follow Z from this edge in both directions until it first meets {x, y, z}. The pieces meet only in x, y, z,
so this gives a path in P2 of length at least two between two vertices of {x, y, z}, and they are distinct
because Z is not contained in P2. This contradicts the previous paragraph.

7. Remarks
How this account differs from the sources. The proof is that of [14], and its presentation here
differs in four places. The disk identity is stated as “one more white face than black”, Lemma 2.2 makes
Euler’s formula applicable to the inside of a cycle, and Proposition 3.9 reads the acyclicity of the selection
off that statement. The sign of a pair is real, as in [4], and is written as a product of local signs. The
weights are heights read along paths of faces. They are the weights that OpenAI construct in the proof
of [14, Lemma 5.3] for one choice of their circulations, and they are Li’s counts of crossings between
half-rays and matching edges [12, Section 4] taken along fixed paths. This choice removes the spokes and
the sliding argument of [14, Section 5.3], and the finite difference of Lemma 4.7 replaces the exponential
sum. The tree of faces Ys is obtained from the opposite sides by planar duality in T; in G it is Li’s black
dual trace [12, Section 6] with one extra leaf.
Which Hamiltonian cycles arise. For fixed t0 and e0, Lemma 3.1 assigns a state sC to every
Hamiltonian cycle C avoiding the edge across e0. C is produced by Proposition 3.9 exactly when sC
is good, and different good states give different cycles. If sC is good, the complement P of C avoids
SsC, because its side in each t ∈A contains sC(t) and its side in t0 is e0. Thus P and PsC are perfect
matchings of the tree YsC, and a tree has at most one perfect matching, so P = PsC. Conversely, when C
comes from a good state s, directing Ps \ {e0} out of s(t) gives outdegree one at each nonroot and zero at
each root. On each rooted tree this is its orientation towards the root, so Lemma 3.1 recovers sC = s.
This also proves that different good states give different cycles. In the example all six such cycles arise.
In the hexagonal prism, whose dual is the hexagonal bipyramid, take a black face as t0 and its side on
the equator as e0; there are three states, two of them good, and four Hamiltonian cycles avoiding the
edge, so only two arise. For the two other sides of t0 there are two such cycles, and both arise.
Why each hypothesis is needed. Bipartite. The colouring of the faces of T is the bipartition of G. It
gives every edge one black face, which makes Qs and D(r, s) consist of distinct edges and gives |A| = |X|.
Tutte’s 46-vertex graph [17] is cubic, planar and 3-connected but not Hamiltonian. The smallest such
graphs have 38 vertices [11].
Planar. On an orientable surface of genus g the same counting gives |V | = k +2−2g, so |X| = |A|−2g
and there are no states when g > 0. The smallest non-Hamiltonian cubic bipartite 3-connected graph is
the 50-vertex graph of Georges and Kelmans [7, 3].
Cubic. Cubicity makes the faces of T triangles, so that a corner has one opposite side, and it gives the
complement of a selection degree two. The incidence graph of the vertices and faces of the triangular
bipyramid has classes of sizes 5 and 6, so it has 11 vertices and no Hamiltonian cycle. It is planar by
drawing each face centre joined to its three corners. Here is also a check of 3-connectivity. The bipyramid
has equatorial vertices p1, p2, p3, apices u, v, and faces up1p2, up2p3, up3p1, vp1p2, vp2p3, vp3p1. Deleting
at most two of its five vertices leaves a connected graph, as is seen directly from the equatorial triangle
and the two apices. If at most one face centre is deleted from the incidence graph, every surviving
bipyramid edge is still represented by a two-edge path through one of its incident face centres, and every
remaining face centre has a surviving corner. If two face centres are deleted, no original vertex is deleted,
and at most one bipyramid edge loses both its incident face centres, since two faces share at most one
side. The bipyramid stays connected after deleting one edge, and the surviving face centres all attach to
it. Thus the incidence graph stays connected after any two vertex deletions.
3-connected. It is used once, to make T simple (Lemma 2.1). Simplicity is used in the density bound,
where a face of H of length two would break bal(Ψ) ≤dΨ −4, and in the pieces of Section 6. Take two new
vertices and three copies of the cube with one edge removed, and join the two ends of the removed edge
in each copy to the two new vertices, one to each, respecting the bipartition. The result is cubic, bipartite
and planar with 26 vertices. To check 2-connectivity, each cube with an edge removed is 2-connected and
joins the two new vertices by a path. Deleting a new vertex leaves the three copies connected through the
other new vertex. Deleting a vertex of a copy leaves that copy connected to at least one new vertex, while
the other two copies still connect the new vertices. Deleting both new vertices leaves three components,
so the graph is 2-connected but has no Hamiltonian cycle. Deciding Hamiltonicity is NP-complete for
2-connected cubic bipartite planar graphs [1].
No separating triangle. Proposition 5.2 uses it once, and it is not necessary: by [4, Theorem 1.2], a
pair exists exactly when no separating triangle has black faces along all three of its sides on the side away
from t0. Two octahedra glued along a face (9 vertices) give the smallest examples, with a pair for one
choice of t0 and none for another of the same colour; an exhaustive check over every face rooting of every
Eulerian sphere triangulation with at most 12 vertices confirms this minimality.

Consequences and open questions. By the equivalence of Gorsky, Steiner and Wiederrecht [8,
Theorem 4.16], Theorem 1.1 implies that every cubic, 3-connected, bipartite Pfaffian graph has a
Hamiltonian cycle through any given path of three edges [14, Corollary 7.2]. Kelmans showed that the
conjecture is equivalent to the statement that for any two distinct edges e, f on a common face there
is a Hamiltonian cycle through e that avoids f (as explained in [9, 2]), so that statement now holds
too. Whether the same holds for two arbitrary distinct edges e, f remains open. The argument above
prescribes only an avoided edge, and the gluing needs exactly that. The Pfaffian consequence uses a
universal equivalence: it passes from the planar theorem to cubic Pfaffian braces through trisums, then
to general cubic 3-connected bipartite Pfaffian graphs through tight-cut decompositions, as proved in [8,
Theorem 4.16].
OpenAI’s accompanying Lean formalisation gives a machine-checked proof of Barnette’s conjecture
[13]. It uses a different route through algebraic duality and does not formally verify every statement or
proof in this exposition, including the two-induced-trees statement of Proposition 2.6(2). Two companion
notes give a primal formulation and an account using two sign-reversing involutions.
