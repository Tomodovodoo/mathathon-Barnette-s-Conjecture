BARNETTE’S CONJECTURE
Lemma 4.2. Let (r, s) be a pair, and direct the edge of Qs in each t ∈A from r(t) to u(t). Then every
vertex of X has outdegree one and every root outdegree zero. Each component of Qs either is a tree
containing exactly one root, or contains no root and exactly one cycle, which is directed. So s is good if
and only if Qs is acyclic, and a good state has exactly one partner.
Proof. The edge of Qs in t joins r(t) and u(t), and r is a bijection onto X, which gives the outdegrees; the
rest of the first two sentences is Lemma 3.7. If s is good, a partner must direct each tree of Qs towards
its root, and r(t) is the tail of the edge of Qs in t. So r is determined. Conversely, directing the trees
towards their roots and taking tails defines a bijection r: A →X with r(t) ̸= s(t), because the edge of
Qs in t avoids s(t).
□
A pair determines a second set of edges, and it gives the second way of grouping the count. Let D(r, s)
be the set of directed edges r(t) →s(t), t ∈A (Figure 7). Every vertex of X is r(t) for one t and s(t′)
for one t′, so it has one outgoing and one incoming edge in D(r, s). The edges come from distinct black
faces, so they are distinct even as undirected edges, and T is simple. Hence D(r, s) is a union of disjoint
directed cycles of T, each of length at least three, covering X, and each face t ∈A supplies one edge to
exactly one of them. The edges of D(r, s) are chosen sides for the state r, and so are the edges of Qs
directed as in Lemma 4.2. So the disk identity applies to every cycle of D(r, s) and to every cycle of Qs.

Two reversals. A pair can be changed along a cycle in two ways.
Lemma 4.3. Let (r, s) be a pair.
(1) Let K be a cycle of Qs, and put ˜r(t) = u(t) for the faces t whose edge lies on K and ˜r(t) = r(t)
otherwise. Then (˜r, s) is a pair, ˜r directs K the other way round, and the same operation applied
to (˜r, s) and K returns (r, s).
(2) Let C be a cycle of D(r, s), and exchange r(t) and s(t) for the faces t whose edge lies on C. The
result is a pair whose set of D-edges is D(r, s) with C reversed.
Proof. (1) By Lemma 4.2 the cycle K is directed by r, so r maps its faces onto the tails of its edges,
which are all its vertices, and u maps them onto the heads, again all its vertices. So ˜r is a bijection, and
˜r(t) = u(t) ̸= s(t). On the faces of K the corners r(t) and u(t) trade roles, so K is reversed and the
operation undoes itself. (2) The tails and the heads of C are both its vertex set, so both new maps are
bijections, and they still differ on every face.
□
