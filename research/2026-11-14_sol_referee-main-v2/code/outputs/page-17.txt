Theorem 6.2. Let T be a coloured triangulation, t0 a face of T and e0 a side of t0. Then T has an
acyclic selection containing e0.
Proof. We use induction on the number of vertices. If T has no separating triangle, exchange the colours
if necessary so that t0 is black. Then T has a pair by Proposition 5.2, a good state by Proposition 4.8,
and an acyclic selection containing e0 by Proposition 3.9.
Otherwise let ∆= xyz be a separating triangle, let D1 be the closed disk bounded by ∆that contains
t0 and D2 the other, and let T1, T2 be the pieces, coloured as in Lemma 6.1. The face t0 is a face of T1,
and by induction T1 has an acyclic selection P1 containing e0. The cap of T1 is bounded by ∆, so P1
contains exactly one side of ∆. Call it e′. The cap of T2 is also bounded by ∆, and by induction T2 has
an acyclic selection P2 containing e′, which is then its only side of ∆. Put P = P1 ∪P2.
P is a selection containing e0. Every face of T is a face of exactly one piece. Say f is a face of T1.
The edges that T1 and T2 share are the sides of ∆, and P2 contains only e′ among them, which is also in
P1. So f has the same sides in P as in P1, namely exactly one, and the same holds for faces of T2.
P is acyclic. By Proposition 2.6(2) applied in Ti, a side of ∆other than e′ has its ends in different
components of Pi. So if e′ = xy, then x and y lie in one component of Pi and z in the other, and the
only path in Pi between two of x, y, z is the edge e′. Suppose Z is a cycle in P. It is not contained in P1
or in P2, so it contains an edge of P2 \ P1, which is not a side of ∆and so has an end strictly inside D2.
Follow Z from this edge in both directions until it first meets {x, y, z}. The pieces meet only in x, y, z,
so this gives a path in P2 of length at least two between two vertices of {x, y, z}, and they are distinct
because Z is not contained in P2. This contradicts the previous paragraph.
□
The prescribed edge is what makes the gluing work: P2 must use the same side of ∆as P1, and an
arbitrary acyclic selection of T2 need not. Read in G, ∆is a cut of three edges, each piece is G with one
side of the cut contracted to a vertex, and the gluing joins two Hamiltonian cycles that use the same two
cut edges. This is the form of the argument in [12, Appendix C]. That it suffices to treat graphs without
such cuts goes back to Kelmans; see [2, Section 5].
