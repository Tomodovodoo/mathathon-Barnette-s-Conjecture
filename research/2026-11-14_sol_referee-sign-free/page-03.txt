3. When a rainbow tree exists
A separating triangle is a 3-cycle of T with vertices on both sides. It has black inner boundary if the
three faces along its sides, on the side away from t0, are black. We use Rado’s theorem in the following
form [7], [8, Exercise 10.26]: subsets X1, . . . , Xm of the ground set of a matroid have an independent
transversal if and only if the union of any j of them has rank at least j.
Theorem 3.1. A rainbow tree exists if and only if no separating triangle of T has black inner boundary.
Proof. For x ∈X let Cx = {e(t, x) : t ∋x}. These sets are disjoint, and Cx is a closed walk through the
white faces at x. By Lemma 2.1 a rainbow tree is an independent transversal of (Cx)x∈X in the cycle
matroid of W, whose rank is n. Two walks Cx and Cy share a white face exactly when xy is an edge of T.
So for J ⊆X the union of the Cx, x ∈J, has one component for each component of the induced graph
T[J], and different components use disjoint sets of white faces. Its rank is w(J) −comp(T[J]), where
w(J) is the number of white faces with a corner in J. Both w and | · | add over the components of T[J],
so by Rado’s theorem a rainbow tree exists if and only if
(3.2)
w(J) ≥|J| + 1
for every nonempty J ⊆X with T[J] connected.
Fix such a J. Let U = V (T) \ J, which contains the roots, and let T[U] have p vertices, m edges and c
components. The connected set J lies in one face Φ of the plane graph T[U]. Every other face of T[U]
contains no vertex of T and no edge of T, since such an edge would join two vertices of U and belong to
T[U]; so it is a face of T. Conversely a face of T with all corners in U is a face of T[U] other than Φ. Let
bU and wU count the black and the white faces of T with all corners in U. Let d be the number of edge
sides on the boundary of Φ, and ς the sum over these sides of +1 or −1 according as the face of T on the
side of Φ is black or white. Then
p −m + (bU + wU + 1) = 1 + c,
2m = 3(bU + wU) + d,
ς = 3(wU −bU).
The first is Euler’s formula and the second counts edge sides. For the third, give every face of T inside Φ
the weight +1 or −1 on each of its three sides. There are n+1−bU black and n+1−wU white faces inside,
the two sides of an edge inside Φ cancel, and the boundary sides remain. Solving, wU = p −c −d/2 + ς/6.

The number ς is a multiple of 3 with the parity of d, and |ς| ≤d. The component of T[U] that contains
t0 has a vertex adjacent to J, so it borders Φ, and it is not a tree, so d ≥3. If c ≥2, the right side of
(3.3) is at least 1 + (2d −12)/6 = (d −3)/3 ≥0. Let c = 1. If d = 4 then ς = 0, if d = 5 then ς ≤3, and
if d ≥6 then 3d −ς ≥2d ≥12; in each case the right side is at least 0. If d = 3, the right side is negative
only for ς = 3. The boundary of Φ is then a triangle ∆, the three faces along it inside Φ are black, and t0
lies on the other side. If the other side contained no vertex, it would be the face t0, and the faces along ∆
inside Φ would be white. So ∆is a separating triangle, and it has black inner boundary. This proves
(3.2) when there is no such triangle.
Conversely let ∆be a separating triangle with black inner boundary, and let J be the set of the I ≥1
vertices inside it. Let B and W ′ count the black and white faces inside ∆. Euler’s formula and the count
of sides for the disk give B + W ′ = 2I + 1, and the signed count of sides gives 3(B −W ′) = 3. So W ′ = I.
Every white face with a corner in J lies inside ∆, so w(J) ≤|J| and the union of the Cx, x ∈J, has rank
less than |J|. By Rado’s theorem there is no rainbow tree.
□
Rado’s theorem is a case of matroid intersection, so a rainbow tree can be found in polynomial time
when one exists.
4. Each rainbow tree lies in one cover
For a 2-factor F of B, let u(t) be the corner of t ∈A that is not joined to t in F. When F is the union
of a pair this is the third corner u(t) of [3]. Let Gu be the digraph on V (T) with an arc v →u(t) for
each of the two corners v of t joined to t in F, for every t ∈A. Its arcs run along the two sides of t at
u(t). Every vertex of X has outdegree two, one arc through each of its two faces in F, and the roots have
outdegree zero (Figure 2).
Lemma 4.1 (orientation). Let F be a 2-factor, let σ choose one edge (x, σ(x)) of F at every x ∈X, and
let (x, σ′(x)) be the other one. Then Fσ is a spanning tree if and only if the n arcs x →u(σ′(x)) contain
no directed cycle. In that case σ′(x) is the parent of x in Fσ rooted at the centre of t0.
