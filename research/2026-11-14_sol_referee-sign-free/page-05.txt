Corollary 4.3. The following are equivalent: T has a pair; T has a rainbow tree; no separating triangle
of T has black inner boundary.
Proof. A rainbow tree lies in a 2-factor by Theorem 4.2(1), and alternate edges along its cycles form a
pair. By Theorem 3.1 it remains to show that a separating triangle ∆with black inner boundary excludes
a 2-factor. Let I vertices lie inside ∆. All their faces lie inside, and there are I + 1 black faces inside by
the proof of Theorem 3.1. The three black faces along the sides of ∆have one corner inside each, and
every face has degree two in a 2-factor. So at most 3 + 2(I −2) = 2I −1 edges of a 2-factor meet the I
inner vertices, and 2I are needed.
□
The equivalence of the first and the third statement is [1, Theorem 1.2]. The exposition proves one
direction, for triangulations without separating triangles, by a flow [3, Proposition 5.2]. There the
max-flow min-cut theorem reduces the existence of a pair to the inequality e(U) −b(U) ≤2|U| −4 for
all sets U of vertices that contain the roots, and Euler’s formula proves it component by component.
Here Rado’s theorem reduces it to (3.2), which concerns only complements of connected sets and follows
from the exact identity (3.3). Both arguments use planarity through one Euler count, and both are
constructive. The route through Rado gives more than a pair. One half of the cover it produces is a
rainbow tree, a selection whose arcs in Gu have no directed cycle. This already has the acyclicity that a
good state needs and lacks only the face condition.
5. The exchange and the parity of the number of good states
Lemma 5.1 (exchange). For a rainbow tree σ and x ∈X there is exactly one face t′ ̸= σ(x) at x such
that σ[x := t′], the map that sends x to t′ and agrees with σ elsewhere, is a rainbow tree. Applying the
exchange at x to σ[x := t′] returns σ.
Proof. The graph Fσ + (σ(x), x) has exactly one cycle Γ. It passes through x and uses two spokes there,
(σ(x), x) and one other, (t′, x). Deleting a spoke at x leaves a spanning tree exactly when the spoke lies
on Γ. The second exchange sees the same graph and the same cycle.
□
Write altσ(x) for t′ (Figure 3). The exchange lowers the load of σ(x) by one and raises the load of
t′, which was at most 1, by one. So an exchange out of a face of load 2 lowers the excess by 2 if t′ was
empty and leaves it unchanged otherwise. Let F be the 2-factor of σ. If giving x its other face in F closes
no directed cycle in Gu, the result is a rainbow tree, so it is the exchange, and the exchange stays in F.
Otherwise the exchange leads to a rainbow tree in another 2-factor.
A walk state is a triple (σ, t, a) of a rainbow tree σ, a face t of load 2 and one of the two vertices of
σ−1(t), the arrival a. The other vertex y is the resident. A step moves the resident to t′ = altσ(y). If t′
was empty, the step stops with the rainbow tree σ[y := t′]. Otherwise t′ had load 1, and the step produces
the walk state step(σ, t, a) = (σ[y := t′], t′, y). The mirror of (σ, t, a) is mir(σ, t, a) = (σ, t, y).
Lemma 5.2 (reversal). If step(S) = S′, then step(mir(S′)) = mir(S). In particular step is injective.
Proof. Let S = (σ, t, a) with resident y, so S′ = (σ′, t′, y) with σ′ = σ[y := t′]. In mir(S′) the resident
is y. By Lemma 5.1 its exchange in σ′ leads back to t, which has load 1 in σ′. So the step produces
(σ, t, y) = mir(S).
□
