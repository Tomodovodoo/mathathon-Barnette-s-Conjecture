GOOD STATES WITHOUT SIGNS
Strict descent. A rainbow tree of positive excess need not have an exchange that lowers the excess.
The smallest examples are in the triangulation with 8 vertices, where 120 rainbow trees have none; up to
12 vertices there are 8782. So a path as in Conjecture 7.2 must cross plateaus of constant excess.
The deterministic walk. The walk of Section 5, started at an arbitrary walk state, need not stop.
Periodic walks exist in triangulations with 38 vertices, with periods 48 and 68, and with 40 vertices, with
period 60. None exists up to 15 vertices. Theorem 5.3 escapes them only because its first walk state has
no predecessor.
Convexity of loads. If the set of load vectors of rainbow trees satisfied the exchange axiom of M-convex
sets, a Hall-type condition would decide whether the vector with all loads 1 belongs to it. The axiom fails
in a triangulation with 12 vertices that has two 2-factors.
The involutions ιt do not help with existence, and they do not connect the good states either: for 13
and 14 vertices there are instances in which the group they generate is not transitive on the good states.
Two sister conjectures. Two other attempts at Theorem 1.1 without signs stop at statements of the
same shape, and at the same obstacle.
Monotone descent on pairs. For a pair (r, s) let q be the number of cycles of Qs, so that s is good when
q = 0. Two moves are available, reversing a cycle of D(r, s) and reversing a cycle of Qs. The conjecture is
that from every pair a good pair can be reached by such moves without ever raising q. It was confirmed
for all pairs up to 16 vertices by two independent programs, and one of them reports all pairs up to 22
vertices. A move that lowers q need not exist, first at 16 vertices, and examples with 21 and 40 vertices
need three and four moves before q falls. Paths that never raise the number of cycles of the cover fail at
16 vertices, so again the cover has to change.
Induction by contraction. Contracting two opposite edges at a vertex of degree four gives a smaller
Eulerian triangulation, and a good state of the smaller one lifts back in exactly one way, which may fail
to be good; a rotation at three faces repairs most failures. Two statements are missing. The first is that
for more than 6 vertices and no separating triangle, some vertex of degree four that is not a root has a
contraction that is simple and has a pair. The second is that where both contractions at such a vertex
have good states, one of them has a good state whose lift or rotated lift is good. No counterexample to
either is known, and the second was confirmed, in a stronger counting form, for all contractions up to
18 vertices. An example with 18 vertices has a good state in a cover with one cycle whose lift fails and
whose only other state in that cover is not good, so a repair must change the cover here too.
In all three settings a number that should reach zero cannot be lowered step by step, and the moves
that keep it constant leave the cover. We know no quantity that decreases along them.
8. What a proof of Step X would give
Suppose Conjecture 7.2 were proved. Theorem 3.1 supplies a rainbow tree whenever no separating
triangle has black inner boundary, the conjecture carries it to a good state, and Theorem 4.2(1) hands
over the partner. That is Theorem 1.1 with no signed count, no weight and no sum over covers. Planarity
would enter through the identity (3.3) and through whatever the proof of the conjecture uses. Barnette’s
conjecture would follow by the remaining steps of [3], which use no cancellation: the passage from a good
state to a Hamiltonian cycle and the gluing along separating triangles. Theorem 5.3 would then give a
second good state.
It would not give a polynomial-time algorithm. The conjecture asserts that a path exists. Strict
descent is false, the deterministic walk can cycle, and nothing bounds the search on a plateau. In the
exhaustive range the plateau search needed depth at most 7, and at most 27 in the sampled examples up
to 120 vertices, which leaves the question open in both directions. It would not count the good states,
and it would not explain the nonvanishing of the signed sum.
Nothing in Section 6 brings the conjecture closer in a direct way, for the reason given after Proposition 7.1.
The place to look is the exchange that leaves the cover. A rainbow tree is an acyclic choice of arcs in
Gu, its excess counts the faces with both arcs or no arc chosen, and correcting a face along a cycle of
the cover works until the new arc closes a directed cycle. That is the same event as a cycle of Qs in the
signed proof, where the disk identity [3, Lemma 3.8] controls it. The disk identity is not used anywhere
in this note, and we expect that a proof of Conjecture 7.2 has to use it.
