GOOD STATES WITHOUT SIGNS: WHAT IS PROVED AND WHERE IT STOPS
Abstract. The proof of Barnette’s conjecture rests on one lemma: a triangulation that has a pair of
disjoint states has a good state, and the only known proofs get it from a signed count. We collect what
can be proved about this lemma with no sign at all. A pair exists exactly when a certain spanning tree
on the white faces exists, by Rado’s theorem and one Euler count; each such tree lies in exactly one
cover; the number of good states is even, by explicit involutions; and inside every cover each of the two
conditions that define a good state can be met alone, by the theorems of Nash-Williams and Edmonds.
Both conditions cannot always be met inside one cover, as a triangulation with 16 vertices shows, so a
proof without signs has to move between covers. We state the conjecture that would finish such a proof,
with the range in which it has been checked and the natural stronger forms that fail.
1. The lemma and the question
We use the notation of the exposition [3]. T is a simple triangulation of the sphere whose faces are
coloured black and white so that every edge has one face of each colour. One black face t0 is drawn as the
outer face, its corners are the roots, A is the set of the other black faces, X the set of vertices that are
not roots, and |A| = |X| = n. A state is a bijection s: A →X with s(t) a corner of t, that is, a perfect
matching of the incidence graph B on A ∪X. Qs is the set of the n sides opposite the corners s(t), and s
is good if Qs is a forest with exactly one root in each component. A good state yields a Hamiltonian
cycle of the cubic graph dual to T [3, Proposition 3.9]. A pair is two states (r, s) with r(t) ̸= s(t) for all t;
then u(t) denotes the third corner of t. The union of the two matchings of a pair is a spanning subgraph
of B with all degrees two, which we call a 2-factor. A 2-factor with c cycles splits into two states in 2c−1
ways, and the 2c ordered pairs obtained from it form one cover in the sense of [3]; we use the two words
for the same object.
The key lemma is [3, Proposition 4.8], due to OpenAI [6].
Theorem 1.1 (good-pair lemma). If T has a pair, then T has a good state.
Every known proof gives each pair a sign, shows that the pairs whose second state is not good cancel,
and shows by a second evaluation that the signed sum is not zero [6, 4, 1, 3]. The proof finds no good
state. It shows that the set of good states is not empty because a sum over it does not vanish. Two things
would be gained from a proof without signs. The first is an algorithm: no polynomial-time construction
of a Hamiltonian cycle in these graphs is known, and a direct argument might provide one. The second is
a reason. The cancellation does not say why a good state exists, in the way Hall’s theorem says why a
matching exists.
This note records how far one gets with no sign. Sections 3 to 6 contain complete proofs. The existence
of a pair, which [3] takes from a flow, is equivalent to the existence of a rainbow tree, a spanning tree on
the white faces that meets one of the two conditions defining a good state (Theorems 3.1 and 4.2). The
good states are paired off by n involutions, so their number is even (Theorem 5.3). Inside every cover,
each of the two conditions can be met alone (Theorems 6.5 and 6.9). Section 7 shows where this stops.
One cover of a triangulation with 16 vertices contains no good state, so every argument that stays inside
a cover fails. What is missing is Conjecture 7.2: from a rainbow tree, exchanges that never raise a certain
excess reach a good state. It holds for every instance with at most 17 vertices. Section 8 says what a
proof of it would give.
Every statement below, proved or not, was also tested on all 262 choices of t0 in all Eulerian
triangulations with at most 12 vertices; Section 9 gives the ranges.
2. Good states as trees on the white faces
Put a centre c(t) in every black face t, the outer face included, and join it to the three corners of t by
spokes. The spoke graph H has the n + 3 vertices of T and the n + 1 centres as vertices and the 3n + 3
spokes as edges. It is connected, because every edge of T lies in a black face. The white-face graph W has
the n + 1 white faces as vertices, and one edge e(t, v) for every spoke (t, v), joining the two white faces
that lie across the two sides of t at v. It can have parallel edges.
