                                          1. Introduction
   The enumeration of planar maps (i.e., connected graphs drawn on the sphere with non-
intersecting edges) has received a lot of attention since the sixties. Four decades of exploration
have resulted into three main enumeration techniques, and two types of results: ﬁrst, a few
remarkable families of maps are counted by very nice and simple numbers; second, and more
generally, many families of maps share the characteristic of having an algebraic generating
function. Meanwhile, many exciting connections have been established between maps and
various branches of mathematics, like Grothendieck’s theory of "dessins d’enfants" or knot
theory, and, most importantly for this paper, between maps and certain branches of physics.
   This paper presents a combinatorial approach for solving two types of physics models on
maps: the Ising and hard particle models. In these models, the vertices of the maps carry
spins or particles; solving these models is equivalent to a weighted enumeration of maps. The
weight is in general a specialization of the Tutte polynomial of the map [44].
   The idea of putting such a model on maps originates in physics, and more precisely in
two-dimensional quantum gravity: in this theory, maps, or random planar lattices, arise as
discrete models of random geometries. Brézin et al., in the steps of t’Hooft [39], developed a
new approach for counting maps, completely diﬀerent from what had been done previously in
combinatorics. This approach, suggested by quantum ﬁeld theory, is based on the evaluation
of matrix integrals with well chosen potentials [6, 16]. An introduction to these techniques,
intended for mathematicians, is presented in [46], and a far reaching account can be found
in [20].
   This approach is extremely powerful: it allows to produce quickly expressions for generating
functions without requiring much invention at the combinatorial level. In particular, the Ising
model on tri- and on tetravalent maps (maps with vertices of degree 3 or 4) was solved via
this approach in the late 80’s, yielding intriguing algebraic generating functions [7, 29, 31]; the
same kind of technique was applied much more recently to the hard particle model on a larger
variety of maps1 [13]. However, the evaluation of the matrix integrals as presented in most
papers is not satisfying from the mathematical point of view: justiﬁcations of the calculations
are often omitted, whereas they involve non-trivial complex analysis and resummation issues.
Moreover, this approach gives little insight on the combinatorial structure of maps and does
not explain the algebraic nature of the generating functions thus obtained.
   The material presented in this paper provides an alternative approach to the Ising and
hard particle models, and has none of the above mentioned drawbacks. It is essentially of a
bijective nature, and only involves elementary combinatorial arguments. We show that certain
families of plane trees are at the heart of both models, and this justiﬁes the algebraicity of
their solutions2. We do not study the singularities of the series we obtain, even though they are

very signiﬁcant from the physics point of view: Many results are in this direction are already
available in [7, 13, 29]; see also the recent detailed study of [40].
   The central objects of our paper are actually bipartite maps, which we enumerate accord-
ing to the degree distribution of their black and white vertices. We thus extend the results
of [9] dealing with constellations, and also the results of [12] on the degree distribution of
(unicoloured) planar maps (these maps are in one-to-one correspondence with bipartite maps
in which all black vertices have degree two). Then, we show that the solutions of the Ising and
hard particle models are closely related to the enumeration of bipartite maps. This connection
is already used in the physics papers [7, 29].
   Our approach provides a new illustration of the principle according to which planar maps
are, in essence, conjugacy classes of trees. This idea was introduced by the second author of
this paper in order to explain bijectively certain nice formulae for the number of maps [34, 35].
Then, it led to a common generalization of formulae of Tutte and Hurwitz [9], before being
adapted to maps satisfying 2- and 3-connectivity constraints [32, 33]. Then in 2002, Bouttier
et al. cleverly built on the same idea to give a bijective derivation of Bender and Canﬁeld’s
solution of the very general problem of counting maps by the degree distribution of their
vertices [4, 12] (see [8] for the equivalence between both solutions).
   Let us mention that the seminal work of Tutte on the enumeration of maps was not based
on bijections, but on recursive decompositions of maps, which led to functional equations
for their generating functions. This type of approach is usually fairly systematic; combined
with the so-called quadratic method and its generalizations, it has provided many algebraicity
results, including recent ones [4, 17, 25, 41, 42]. It yields short self-contained proofs, relying
only on elementary decompositions and algebraic calculus in the realm of formal power series.
However, as far as we know, there has been very few successful attempts to apply this method
to the enumeration of maps carrying an additional structure, like an Ising model. Roughly
speaking, the standard decompositions of maps still provide certain functional equations, but
these are much harder to solve that in the previous cases. This is perfectly illustrated by the
tour de force achieved by Tutte, who spent 10 years solving the equation he had established
for the chromatic polynomial of triangulations [43].
   For the sake of completeness, let us mention a few additional results and techniques in map
enumeration. The ﬁrst bijective proofs are due to Cori [18] and later to Arquès [3]. Some
models involving self-avoiding walks on maps can essentially be solved without matrix inte-
grals [21], by using enumeration results due to Tutte. Then, a cluster expansion method has
been developed for a general model of spins with neighbor interactions on maps [30]. How-
ever, this provides only partial qualitative results and does not allow to recover the critical
exponents of the hard particle and Ising models (whereas they can be derived from the gen-
erating functions obtained either with our approach or with matrix integrals). Finally, the
enumerative theory of ramiﬁed coverings of the sphere has led a number of authors to consider
a very reﬁned enumeration of maps on higher genus surfaces, in which the degrees of vertices
and faces are taken into account. The relevant methods then rely on the encoding of maps by
permutations [19] and lead to expressions involving characters of the symmetric group [26, 28].
   The structure of the paper is outlined at the end of the next section, after the description
of our results.

                              2. A glimpse at the results
   To begin with, let us recall a few deﬁnitions and conventions. A planar map is a 2-cell
decomposition of the oriented sphere into vertices (0-cells), edges (1-cells) and faces (2-cells).
In more vernacular terms, it is a connected multigraph drawn on a sphere with non-intersecting
edges. Loops and multiple edges are allowed. Two maps are isomorphic if there exists an
orientation preserving homeomorphism of the sphere that sends one onto the other. The
4                            M. BOUSQUET-MÉLOU AND G. SCHAEFFER


degree of a vertex is the number of incident half-edges, which means that a loop counts twice.
The degree of a face is the length of its boundary, where each edge has length 1. A map is
rooted if one of its edges is distinguished and oriented. In this case, the map is drawn on the
plane in such a way the inﬁnite face lies to the right of the root edge. All the maps considered
in this paper are planar, rooted, and considered up to isomorphisms. An example is provided
in Figure 1.1; this map is tetravalent, meaning that all vertices have degree 4. In the physics
literature, authors often consider unrooted maps; but they count them with a symmetry factor
which makes the problem equivalent to counting rooted maps, up to a diﬀerentiation of the
(unrooted) generating function; see e.g. [36, Sec. 5.2.3] for details.

2.1. The Ising and hard particle models on maps
   In combinatorial terms, the solution of the Ising model on planar maps (in a magnetic ﬁeld)
is equivalent to counting maps having vertices of two types (white and black): one has to
count them, not only by their number of edges and vertices of each colour, but also by the
number of frustrated edges, that is, edges that are adjacent to a white vertex and to a black
one.
   The tools developed in this paper allow us to solve the Ising model on any class of maps
subject to degree conditions. The associated generating functions are algebraic as soon as
the degrees of the vertices are bounded. Here is, for instance, the result that we obtain for
“quasi-tetravalent” maps: maps having only vertices of degree 4, except for the root vertex
which has degree 2 (Figure 1.2).

2.2. Bipartite maps and blossom trees
   Our method for solving the Ising and hard particle models uses a detour via the enumeration
of fully frustrated maps, better known as bipartite maps. Their enumeration will be based on
a correspondence between these maps and certain trees, called blossom trees. These trees
have an algebraic generating function as soon as the degrees of their vertices are bounded. In
particular, the series P of Proposition 2.1 will be shown to count certain blossom trees.

   Let us give a brief description of these blossom trees. An example is provided by Figure 3.
These trees are one-face maps, and are properly bicoloured: all the neighbours of a black vertex
are white, and vice-versa. In addition to the standard vertices and edges, blossom trees carry
half-edges, which are called leaves when they hang from a white vertex, and buds otherwise.
Leaves are represented in our ﬁgures by short segments, while buds are represented by black
outgoing arrows. The trees are rooted at a leaf or a bud, and the vertex attached to this
half-edge is called the root vertex. Finally, we deﬁne the charge of a tree to be the diﬀerence
between the number of leaves and the number of buds it contains; the root half-edge does not
count. The charge at a vertex is the charge of the subtree rooted at this vertex. We impose
the following charge conditions on the non-root vertices of blossom trees: white vertices have
a nonnegative charge, while black vertices have charge at most one. The notion of charge was
introduced by Bouttier et al. in [12] for the enumeration of usual planar maps, and turns out
to be also useful in the bipartite case.

Outline of the paper. In Section 3, we describe a general connection between maps and
trees: one transforms a map into a tree by cutting some of its edges into two half-edges.
Conversely, merging half-edges of a tree gives a planar map. In Section 4, we show that these
transformations, restricted to certain classes of maps and trees (called balanced trees) are one-
to-one. Balanced trees are then enumerated in Section 5: this proves our central Theorem 2.3
above. Finally, Sections 6 and 7 are respectively devoted to applications of this theorem to
the solution of the hard particle and Ising models.

3.1. Maps with legs and blossom trees
   Take a bipartite planar map M rooted at a black vertex v of degree 2. Such maps are
exactly those we wish to count (Theorem 2.3). Let us consider that each of the two edges
incident to v is made of two half-edges. Delete v and the two half-edges attached to it. Two
cases occur (Figure 5):
      • either we obtain an ordered pair of maps, each of them carrying a half-edge (or leg),
        on which it is rooted,
      • or we obtain a single map with two half-edges, and root this map at the half-edge
        belonging to the root edge of M .

   The above decomposition explains why, as in [12], we shall be interested in generalized
bipartite maps that do not only consist of the usual edges and vertices, but also contain a
number of half-edges in their inﬁnite face (when drawn on the plane), and are rooted at one of
these half-edges. Let us insist on the distinction between a half-edge, which is incident to one
vertex only, and a dangling edge, which is incident to two vertices, one of which has degree
one. From now on, in this section and the next two, all maps are bipartite (with a proper
bicolouring) and rooted at a half-edge.

   We need to introduce more terminology. In passing, we shall reformulate slightly the deﬁ-
nition of blossom trees given in Section 2. The vertex adjacent to the root half-edge is called
the root vertex of the map. Half-edges that hang from black vertices are called buds and are
represented in our ﬁgures by black outgoing arrows. Half-edges that hang from white vertices
are called leaves and are represented by short segments. The degree distribution of a map is
the pair of partitions (λ, µ) such that λ gives the degree distribution of white vertices and µ
gives the degree distribution of black vertices. Half-edges are included in the degree of the
vertex they are attached to. A map with nb buds and nℓ leaves satisﬁes nb + |λ| = nℓ + |µ|
where |λ| denotes the sum of the parts of λ. The 1-leg map of Figure 6 has degree distribution
λ = 22 · 3 · 4 · 5, µ = 2 · 42 · 5.
   Let us now deﬁne two important subclasses of maps. A k-leg map is a map with exactly k
leaves and no bud; hence a k-leg map is rooted at one of its leaves. A tree is a map with only
one face (and an arbitrary number of buds and leaves), rooted at a half-edge as all maps in
this section. In our ﬁgures, the root is usually at the top. The total charge of a tree is the
diﬀerence between its number of leaves and its number of buds; the root half-edge is counted.
The charge of a tree is the same diﬀerence, but the root half-edge is not counted. Hence the
charge and total charge always diﬀer by ±1.
   Take a tree T and an edge e of this tree. Cut e into two half-edges: this leaves two subtrees,
rooted at these half-edges. The subtree that does not contain the root of T is called the lower
subtree of T at e. Let Te• denote the subtree containing the black endpoint of e and Te◦ the
other one. The charges c◦ of Te◦ and c• of Te• satisfy c◦ + c• = c, where c is the total charge
10                           M. BOUSQUET-MÉLOU AND G. SCHAEFFER


of T . The black charge rule is satisﬁed at e if the subtree Te• has charge at most one. The
white charge rule is satisﬁed at e if the subtree Te◦ has a nonnegative charge.
   A tree is T called a blossom tree if all its lower subtrees satisfy the charge rule corresponding
to their colour. An example is displayed in Figure 3, right. Note that T itself is not one of
its lower subtrees, so that there is no condition on the total charge. This is in agreement with
the original deﬁnition of blossom trees in Section 2.2. Recall that the series Wi and Bi deﬁned
by (2.1)–(2.4) count blossom trees of charge i rooted at a leaf and a bud, respectively.
   The following lemma, dealing with blossom trees of total charge 1, is straightforward. As
we shall see, these trees are usually much simpler to handle than trees of larger charge.

Lemma 3.1. Let T be a tree of total charge one, and let e be an edge of T . Then the black
charge rule is satisﬁed at e if and only if the white charge rule is satisﬁed at e.
   If, in addition, T is a blossom tree, then both charge rules are satisﬁed at every edge. Hence
re-rooting T at another half-edge yields again a blossom tree.

3.2. Balanced trees and their closure
   In this section we deﬁne the closure as a mapping φ from certain trees with total charge
k ≥ 1 to k-leg maps. This closure is the same as in earlier texts using the idea of conjugacy
classes of trees [9, 12, 32, 33, 34, 35].
   Let T be a tree with total charge k ≥ 1. Its half-edges form a cyclic sequence around the
tree in counterclockwise direction. Buds and leaves can be matched in this cyclic sequence as
if they were respectively opening and closing brackets. More precisely, ﬁrst match the pairs
made of a bud and a leaf that are immediately consecutive in the cyclic sequence. Then, forget
matched buds and leaves, and repeat until there is no more possible matching. In view of the
number of buds and leaves in the original cyclic sequence, exactly k leaves remain unmatched.
These leaves are called the single leaves of T . A tree is balanced if its root is single; this forces
the root to be a leaf.
   The closure φ(T ) of a balanced tree T with charge k ≥ 1 is obtained by merging each
matched pair bud/leaf into an single edge (going from the bud to the leaf in counterclockwise
direction around the tree). The root remains unchanged, and is thus a leg of the map. An
example is shown in Figure 6. More generally, we will sometimes apply the same closure
operation to a tree T with total charge k ≥ 1 in which the root half-edge is not a single leaf
(and is possibly a bud): then the resulting map is (exceptionally) not rooted at a leg, but at
the edge that was created when matching the root.
   We have the following property.

Proposition 3.2. Let T be a balanced tree having total charge k ≥ 1 and degree distribution
(λ, µ). Then φ(T ) is a k-leg map with degree distribution (λ, µ).

   There is an alternative description of the closure as an iterative process, illustrated by
Figure 6: start from a balanced tree T and walk around the inﬁnite face in counterclockwise
order; each time a bud is immediately followed by a leaf in the cyclic sequence of half-edges,
merge them into an edge in counterclockwise direction (this creates a new ﬁnite face that
encloses no unmatched half-edge); stop when all buds have been matched. Observe that this
process may in general require to turn several times around the tree. A more eﬃcient method
is to use a stack (that is, a ﬁrst-in-last-out waiting line): store buds in the stack as they are
met and match leaves as they are met with the ﬁrst bud available in the stack. While this is
algorithmically more eﬀective, we shall content ourselves with the previous simpler description.
   Observe that two distinct trees may yield the same map, as illustrated in Figure 7. Note
however that the ﬁrst tree is not a blossom tree. The blossoming condition will play a key role
in our construction of bijections between maps and trees.

3.3. The opening of a k-leg map
   In this section we deﬁne the opening ψ as a mapping from k-leg maps to trees with total
charge k.
   The opening of a map is the result of an iterative procedure: start from a k-leg map M and
walk around the inﬁnite face in counterclockwise order, starting from the root; each time a
non-separating3 edge has just been visited from its black endpoint to its white endpoint, cut
this edge into two half-edges: a bud at the black endpoint and a leaf at the white endpoint;
proceed until all edges are separating edges. An example is shown in Figure 8. The opening
process cannot get stuck: as long as there are non-separating edges, a positive even number
of them are incident to the inﬁnite face; half of them are oriented from black to white in
counterclockwise direction. The ﬁnal result is then a tree rooted at a leaf.

   3An edge is separating if its deletion disconnects the map, non-separating otherwise; a map is a tree if and

   In view of the number of buds and leaves created, the image T = ψ(M ) of a k-leg map is
a tree with total charge k. Moreover, the pairs of buds and leaves created by the opening are
in correspondence in the matching procedure of the tree described in Section 3.2. Hence the
tree is balanced, and we have the following proposition.
Proposition 3.3. The closure is inverse to the opening: for any k-leg map M , φ(ψ(M )) = M .
   The above proposition, combined with Proposition 3.2, guarantees that the opening ψ is a
bijection between maps with k legs and their image by ψ. The problem is now to characterize
this image, which is a set of balanced trees with total charge k. In the next section, we
characterize the image by ψ of several families of maps, including those having k = 1 or k = 2
legs.

4.2. Two-leg maps and other families
  We now present a version of Theorem 4.1 for 2-leg maps.
Theorem 4.2. Closure and opening are inverse bijections between balanced blossom trees of
total charge 2 and 2-leg maps. Moreover, they preserve the degree distribution (λ, µ).
Proof. Take a balanced blossom tree T of total charge 2. Beyond its root leaf r, another leaf,
say e1 , is single. It ends up in the inﬁnite face of the map φ(T ) when closing the edges of T .
The idea is to replace one of the single leaves of T by a black vertex of degree 1, so as to
obtain a tree with total charge 1, and then apply Theorem 4.1. However, transforming the
leaf e1 in this way may yield a tree that is not a blossom tree, as can be easily seen. What
we do instead is to replace the root leaf r by a black vertex of degree 1 – more precisely, by a
dangling edge e0 ending at marked black vertex – and choose e1 as the new root. We obtain
a tree T ′ of total charge 1, rooted at a leaf and carrying a marked black vertex of degree 1.
   We claim that T ′ is a blossom tree. First, the charge condition is clearly satisﬁed at the new
edge e0 . Then, for any edge e of T ′ that was already in T , one of the subtrees Te′◦ and Te′• is a
lower subtree in T , and thus satisﬁes the charge condition. By the ﬁrst part of Lemma 3.1, this
implies that T ′ is a blossom tree. Moreover, the pairs of buds and leaves that are matched by
closure are the same in T ′ as in T (the closure does not depend on the root). In particular e0
and e1 remain in the inﬁnite face in φ(T ′ ), and T ′ is balanced.

   We claim that the transformation f that transforms T into T ′ is a bijection between balanced
blossom trees of total charge 2 and balanced blossom trees T ′ of total charge 1 with a marked
black vertex of degree 1 that ends up in the inﬁnite face of φ(T ′ ) when the edges of T ′ are
closed: indeed, for any tree T ′ satisfying the latter properties, one obtains a balanced blossom
tree T of total charge 2 by replacing the marked black vertex by a leaf which becomes the
new root; the proof that T is a blossom tree is similar to the proof that T ′ was a blossom tree
above.
   Now let us apply Theorem 4.1: the closing procedure φ induces a bijection between the

root: the resulting map M is precisely φ(T ), where T = f −1 (T ′ ), and M has the same degree

Theorem 4.3. Let m ≥ 3 be an integer. Closure and opening are inverse bijections between

Lemma 4.4. Let T be a tree in which the degrees of all vertices are multiples of m. Then m
divides the total charge of T .

if its charge is at least m − 1 (a stronger condition). A lower subtree with a black root vertex
satisﬁes the charge rule if and only if its total charge is at most m (a weaker condition).

the vertex distribution of T . Observe that m divides |λ| and |µ|. Then m also divides the
total charge of T , because the latter is the diﬀerence between the numbers nℓ of leaves and nb
of buds, which satisfy nb + |λ| = nℓ + |µ| (as observed in Section 3.1).
   Now the vertices of any lower subtree of T have a degree that is a multiple of m. Thus if T ◦
is a lower subtree of T with a white root vertex that satisﬁes the charge rule, the total charge
of T ◦ is at least 1, hence at least m, hence the charge of T ◦ is in fact at least m − 1. If T • is
a lower subtree of T with a black root vertex and a charge at most m, its total charge is at
most m − 1, hence at most 0 by the ﬁrst part of the lemma; this means that the charge of T •
is at most 1, so that the charge rule is satisﬁed.                                                

Lemma 5.3. Let T be a blossom tree with total charge k ≥ 1 and let e be an edge of T . Denote
by c• the charge of Te• and by c◦ the charge of Te◦ , so that c• + c◦ = k.
      • If the root of T belongs to Te◦ then the black charge rule is satisﬁed at e (that is, c• ≤ 1)
        and the charge of Te◦ is at least k−1: c◦ ≥ k−1. Hence the white charge rule is satisﬁed
        at e as well.
      • If the root of T belongs to Te• then the white charge rule is satisﬁed at e (that is, c◦ ≥ 0)
        and the charge of Te• is at most k: c• ≤ k. Hence the black charge rule may not be
        satisﬁed at e if k ≥ 2.
If c• ≥ 2 the edge e is called weak. Weak edges may only exist if k ≥ 2.
  The above lemma implies that if we re-root a blossom tree “on the wrong side” of a weak
edge, the result will not be a blossom tree. This can be reformulated as follows.
Lemma 5.4. Let T be a blossom tree with total charge k ≥ 1, and let T ′ be obtained by re-
rooting T at a half-edge e. Then T ′ is a blossom tree if and only if there is no weak edge on
the path from the root of T to e. In particular, if k = 1 then T ′ is always a blossom tree, as
stated in Lemma 3.1.
   This leads to the notion of core, introduced in [12]: let T be a blossom tree with total charge
k ≥ 1, and let S be the set of weak edges of T . The core of T is the connected component of
T \ S containing the root. Lemma 5.4 can be reformulated by saying that re-rooting a blossom
tree of positive total charge gives a blossom tree if and only if the new root is chosen in the
core. In this case, the core of the new tree coincides with that of the original one.
   Lemma 5.3 implies that a blossom tree of total charge k ≥ 1 satisﬁes the white charge rule
at every edge. Lemma 3.1 implies that the converse is true when k = 1. The following lemma
relies on the notion of core to state some kind of reciprocal when k = 2. This will be useful in
the proof of the second part of Theorem 5.1.
Lemma 5.5. Let T be a tree of total charge 2 such that the white charge condition holds at
every edge. Then there exists a leaf ℓ of T such that re-rooting T at ℓ gives a blossom tree.
Proof. We ﬁrst generalize the notion of weak edges and core to trees T satisfying the conditions
of the lemma. We say that an edge e of T is weak if it has charge 2 at its black endpoint (and
thus charge 0 at the other end). For all non-weak edges, both charge rules hold. Let S be the
set of weak edges of T . We claim that, when deleting all edges of S, we obtain a collection
of trees such that all trees but one have total charge 0; the last tree has total charge 2. This
can be proved by induction of the number of weak edges: let e be a weak edge of T such that
                    THE DEGREE DISTRIBUTION IN BIPARTITE PLANAR MAPS                              19


there is no further weak edge in Te◦ . Then deleting e leaves a component of total charge 0
(this is Te◦ without its root leaf), and a tree of total charge 2 (this is Te• without its root bud)
whose weak edges are those of T , except e. We then repeat the procedure to conclude. We
deﬁne the core of T as the unique component of T \ S with total charge 2.
   Observe that if T is also a blossom tree, the deﬁnition of weak edges coincides with that of
Lemma 5.3. Moreover, if e is a weak edge then the root of T belongs to Te• by Lemma 5.3.
This shows that both notions of core coincide as well.
   Let us now return to a tree T that satisﬁes the white charge rule at every edge but is not
necessarily a blossom tree. Let ℓ be one of the leaves of the core (there are at least 2 of them),
and let T ′ be obtained by re-rooting T at ℓ. We claim that T ′ is a blossom tree. Since the white
charge rule is satisﬁed at every edge of T (hence of T ′ ), and the black charge rule at every
non-weak edge, we just need to consider weak edges, and to prove that the lower subtree of T ′
at a weak edge e is always Te◦ . If this were not the case, deleting e would leave a component
of total charge 2 not containing the core, which is impossible. This concludes the proof.         
5.3. When all degrees are multiples of m: proof of Theorem 5.2
   As already exempliﬁed by the proof of Lemma 4.4, the assumptions of Theorem 5.2 make
it more diﬃcult to violate the charge rule. Let us prove that, under these assumptions, a
blossom tree T with total charge m has T as its core. Assume that T contains a weak edge e.
This is only possible if m ≥ 2. Then Te◦ has a (nonnegative) charge c◦ = m − c• ≤ m − 2 (see
Lemma 5.3). Equivalently, Te◦ is a blossom tree with total charge c = c◦ + 1 in the interval
[1, m − 1]. This is impossible by Lemma 4.4, since all vertex degrees of Te◦ are multiples of m.
Hence T has no weak edge.
   We can now prove Theorem 5.2 by adapting the argument used above to prove the ﬁrst part
of Theorem 5.1. A tree belonging to W   cm−1 is either balanced (that is, belongs to Wc∗ ) or
